The basics

Q01
What is the difference between WHERE and HAVING?
Answer
WHERE filters rows before they are grouped. HAVING filters groups after aggregation. That ordering is why you cannot put an aggregate in WHERE — at the point WHERE runs, the groups do not exist yet.

Say the execution order out loud and the answer becomes obvious: FROM → WHERE → GROUP BY → HAVING → SELECT → ORDER BY.
-- wrong: COUNT(*) does not exist yet at WHERE
SELECT department FROM employees
WHERE COUNT(*) > 45 GROUP BY department;

-- right
SELECT department, COUNT(*) AS n
FROM employees GROUP BY department
HAVING COUNT(*) > 45;
Q02
What is the order SQL actually executes a query in?
Answer
Not the order you write it. FROM → JOIN → WHERE → GROUP BY → HAVING → SELECT → DISTINCT → ORDER BY → LIMIT.

Two consequences that get asked as follow-ups: a column alias created in SELECT cannot be used in WHERE (SELECT has not run yet) but can be used in ORDER BY (which runs after). And WHERE cannot see an aggregate.
Q03
DELETE, TRUNCATE and DROP — what is the difference?
Answer
DELETE removes rows, can take a WHERE, is logged row by row, and can be rolled back. TRUNCATE removes every row, takes no WHERE, is much faster because it deallocates pages rather than logging rows, and resets identity counters. DROP removes the table itself — structure, indexes, constraints, the lot.

DELETE and TRUNCATE are DML/DDL respectively; the practical answer an interviewer wants is DELETE is selective and reversible, TRUNCATE empties, DROP destroys.
Q04
What is the difference between CHAR and VARCHAR?
Answer
CHAR is fixed length and pads with spaces; VARCHAR stores only what you put in, plus a length byte or two. Use CHAR when the value genuinely is always the same width — a country code, a Y/N flag — and VARCHAR for anything else. The trap in practice is that trailing spaces in CHAR break equality comparisons people expect to work.
Q05
What does DISTINCT do, and what is the cost?
Answer
Removes duplicate rows from the result. Two things to say beyond the definition: it applies to the whole row of selected columns, not just the first one; and it has to sort or hash the result to find duplicates, so on a large table it is not free. If you are reaching for DISTINCT to fix duplicates, often the real problem is a join that is fanning out rows.

Joins

Q06
Explain the different types of join.
Answer
INNER — only rows that match on both sides.
LEFT — every row from the left table, plus matches; NULLs where there is none.
RIGHT — the mirror image.
FULL OUTER — everything from both, NULLs on whichever side is missing.
CROSS — every row against every row, so 100 × 50 gives 5,000. Almost always accidental.
SELF — a table joined to itself, which is how you handle a manager_id pointing at employee_id.
SELECT e.name, d.department
FROM employees e
LEFT JOIN departments d ON e.dept_id = d.dept_id;
Q07
How do you find rows that exist in one table but not the other?
Answer
A LEFT JOIN with an IS NULL check on the right-hand side — the anti-join. This is a very common live-coding ask.

Say why it works: the LEFT JOIN keeps every left row, and the only rows where the right-hand columns are NULL are the ones that found no match.
SELECT e.*
FROM employees e
LEFT JOIN departments d ON e.dept_id = d.dept_id
WHERE d.dept_id IS NULL;
Q08
Your join returned more rows than the left table had. Why?
Answer
Because the join key is not unique on the right-hand side. One left row matched several right rows, so it was duplicated — the classic fan-out.

The diagnosis: count rows before and after, then check the key with SELECT key, COUNT(*) FROM t GROUP BY key HAVING COUNT(*) > 1. This is the single most useful join debugging answer a fresher can give.
Q09
What is the difference between UNION and UNION ALL?
Answer
UNION stacks two result sets and removes duplicates. UNION ALL stacks them and keeps everything.

UNION ALL is faster because it does not have to deduplicate. Both require the same number of columns with compatible types. If you know the two sets cannot overlap, UNION ALL is the right choice and saying so shows judgement.
Q10
What is the difference between a JOIN and a UNION?
Answer
A JOIN adds columns — it widens the result by matching rows side by side. A UNION adds rows — it stacks two result sets with the same shape on top of each other. Same rows or same columns is the question to ask yourself.

Aggregation and grouping

Q11
What is the difference between COUNT(*), COUNT(column) and COUNT(DISTINCT column)?
Answer
COUNT(*) counts rows, including rows that are entirely NULL in the column you care about.
COUNT(column) counts non-NULL values in that column.
COUNT(DISTINCT column) counts distinct non-NULL values.

The gap between the first two is your missing data count, and that is usually the point of the question.
SELECT COUNT(*)            AS rows,
       COUNT(salary)       AS have_salary,
       COUNT(*) - COUNT(salary) AS missing
FROM employees;
Q12
How do aggregate functions treat NULL?
Answer
They skip it. AVG, SUM, MIN, MAX and COUNT(column) all ignore NULLs rather than treating them as zero. So AVG over a column with two blanks divides by the non-null count, not the row count.

That is usually correct, but it is silent — if you want blanks treated as zero you have to say so with COALESCE.
Q13
Find duplicate rows in a table.
Answer
GROUP BY the columns that define identity, then HAVING COUNT(*) > 1. The only real decision is which columns count as the identity of a row — an email alone, an email and a date, or the whole row. There is no universally right answer; the business decides, and a candidate who asks rather than assumes is doing well.

Add GROUP_CONCAT(id) and the result tells you which rows collided, which is what you need on screen before deleting anything.

The follow-up is always now delete them, keeping one — a self-join on the identity columns with a.id > b.id. MySQL will not let you delete from a table you are also selecting from in a subquery, which is why the self-join is needed at all.
-- the duplicate keys
SELECT emp_id, COUNT(*) AS n
FROM employees GROUP BY emp_id HAVING COUNT(*) > 1;

-- the duplicate ROWS, keeping the first of each
SELECT * FROM (
  SELECT *, ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY emp_id ORDER BY emp_id) AS rn
  FROM employees
) t WHERE rn > 1;
Q14
Show each department's headcount and average salary, highest average first.
Answer
A straight GROUP BY, and the follow-ups are where the marks are.

Every non-aggregated column in SELECT must appear in GROUP BY — MySQL will sometimes let you break that rule and silently return an arbitrary row's value, which is worth knowing because it is a real source of wrong numbers.

The alias can be used in ORDER BY but not in WHERE, because SELECT runs after WHERE and before ORDER BY. ROUND is not cosmetic either — an unrounded average pasted into a report is how fifteen decimal places reach a stakeholder.

And the thing to probe: departments with no employees do not appear at all, because GROUP BY can only group rows that exist. If the report needs every department listed, you have to drive from the departments table with a LEFT JOIN.
SELECT department,
       COUNT(*)            AS headcount,
       ROUND(AVG(salary),2) AS avg_salary
FROM employees
GROUP BY department
ORDER BY avg_salary DESC;

Subqueries and CTEs

Q15
What is a subquery, and what is a correlated subquery?
Answer
A subquery is a query inside another query; it runs once and hands its result up. A correlated subquery references a column from the outer query, so it runs once per outer row — which is why it can be slow.

If asked to optimise one, the usual answer is to rewrite it as a JOIN or a window function.
-- correlated: runs per row
SELECT name, salary FROM employees e
WHERE salary > (SELECT AVG(salary) FROM employees
                WHERE department = e.department);
Q16
What is a CTE and why use one?
Answer
A Common Table Expression is a named temporary result defined with WITH, available to the query that follows.

Use it for readability — a three-level nested subquery becomes three named steps you can read top to bottom. It can also be referenced more than once in the same query, which a subquery cannot. A recursive CTE additionally walks hierarchies like an org chart.
WITH dept_avg AS (
  SELECT department, AVG(salary) AS avg_sal
  FROM employees GROUP BY department
)
SELECT e.name, e.salary, d.avg_sal
FROM employees e
JOIN dept_avg d ON e.department = d.department
WHERE e.salary > d.avg_sal;
Q17
CTE or subquery or temp table — when would you pick each?
Answer
Subquery for something small and used once. CTE when it makes the query readable or you need the result twice in one statement. Temp table when the intermediate result is large, expensive to compute, and needed across several statements — and because you can index a temp table, which you cannot do to a CTE.

Window functions

Q18
What is a window function, and how does it differ from GROUP BY?
Answer
A window function computes across a set of rows without collapsing them. GROUP BY turns ten rows into one; a window function returns all ten with an extra column.

That is the whole distinction, and it is the most common window-function interview question.
SELECT name, department, salary,
       AVG(salary) OVER (PARTITION BY department) AS dept_avg,
       salary - AVG(salary) OVER (PARTITION BY department) AS gap
FROM employees;
Q19
ROW_NUMBER, RANK and DENSE_RANK — what is the difference?
Answer
On the values 100, 90, 90, 80:

ROW_NUMBER → 1, 2, 3, 4. Always distinct, ties broken arbitrarily.
RANK → 1, 2, 2, 4. Ties share a rank and the next value skips.
DENSE_RANK → 1, 2, 2, 3. Ties share, and nothing is skipped.

Pick ROW_NUMBER for deduplication, DENSE_RANK for 'the Nth distinct value'.
Q20
Find the top 3 earners in each department.
Answer
The canonical window-function question. PARTITION BY the group, ORDER BY the measure, filter on the rank in an outer query — you cannot filter a window function in WHERE, because WHERE runs before SELECT.
SELECT * FROM (
  SELECT name, department, salary,
         DENSE_RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY department
                            ORDER BY salary DESC) AS rnk
  FROM employees
) t
WHERE rnk <= 3;
Q21
What do LAG and LEAD do?
Answer
LAG reaches backwards to an earlier row in the window; LEAD reaches forwards. Both take an optional offset and an optional default, so LAG(revenue, 12, 0) gives you the same month last year, or 0 where there is none.

This is how you do period-on-period change without a self-join, and it is the single most useful window function in reporting work.

Three details that come up as follow-ups. PARTITION BY is almost always needed, or one customer's last row leaks into the next customer's first. The first row of each partition has no previous row, so LAG returns NULL — which is correct and should not be COALESCEd to zero, because 'no previous month' and 'zero growth' are different facts. And when you divide by the lagged value, guard it with NULLIF(prev, 0).
SELECT month, revenue,
       LAG(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY month) AS prev_month,
       revenue - LAG(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY month) AS change
FROM monthly;
Q22
How would you calculate a running total?
Answer
SUM(x) OVER (ORDER BY ...). The ORDER BY inside the window is what makes it cumulative — without it you get the same grand total on every row, which is a different and also useful thing.

The detail worth knowing is the frame. Supplying ORDER BY gives you a default frame of RANGE BETWEEN UNBOUNDED PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW, which is exactly a running total, so you rarely have to write it. You only spell the frame out when you want something else — a trailing 7-row moving average is ROWS BETWEEN 6 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW.

One subtlety that catches people: the default frame is RANGE, not ROWS, so tied values in the ORDER BY all get the same cumulative figure. If two orders share a date, a running total by date jumps past both together. Add a tie-breaker column, or switch to ROWS.
SELECT join_date, salary,
       SUM(salary) OVER (ORDER BY join_date) AS running_total
FROM employees;

NULLs, keys and design

Q23
Why does = NULL not work?
Answer
Because NULL means unknown, not a value. Comparing anything to an unknown gives unknown, not true — so the row is never returned, and no error is raised. Use IS NULL and IS NOT NULL.

The same logic explains why NULL = NULL is not true, and why COUNT(column) skips them.
Q24
What is the difference between a primary key and a unique key?
Answer
Both enforce uniqueness. A primary key cannot contain NULL and there is only one per table. A unique key allows NULLs (usually one, depending on the engine) and you can have several per table.

Practical test for whether a column can be a key: distinct = count = number of rows, and no NULLs. All-values-different is not sufficient on its own.
Q25
What is a foreign key?
Answer
A column that points at the primary key of another table, which is what makes a join meaningful. It also enforces referential integrity — you cannot insert a dept_id that does not exist in departments, and depending on the rule you cannot delete a department that still has employees.
Q26
What is normalisation, and when would you denormalise?
Answer
Organising tables to remove redundancy. 1NF atomic values, no repeating groups. 2NF 1NF plus no partial dependency on part of a composite key. 3NF 2NF plus no transitive dependency — non-key columns depend only on the key.

You denormalise deliberately in reporting and warehouse tables, where read speed matters more than update anomalies and a join per query is too expensive.
Q27
What is an index, and why not index everything?
Answer
A structure that lets the database find rows without scanning the table — conceptually the index at the back of a book.

You do not index everything because each index has to be maintained on every insert, update and delete, and it takes storage. Indexes pay off on columns you filter or join on, and on high-cardinality columns; an index on a Y/N flag rarely helps.
Q28
What does COALESCE do?
Answer
Returns the first non-NULL argument from any number of arguments, so it is how you give a NULL a display value or fall back through a chain of candidate columns. It is standard SQL, which is why you should prefer it to MySQL's two-argument IFNULL.

The warning that matters more than the definition: it changes your statistics. AVG(COALESCE(salary, 0)) treats a missing salary as a salary of zero and drags the mean down — which is almost never what anybody meant. AVG(salary) on its own ignores NULLs, and that is usually right.

So the rule is: coalesce for display, not inside an aggregate, unless you have decided deliberately that missing really does mean zero and have said so in the report.
SELECT name,
       COALESCE(salary, 0)          AS salary_or_zero,
       COALESCE(department,'Unknown') AS dept
FROM employees;

Design, keys and constraints

Q29
What are DDL, DML, DCL and TCL?
Answer
The four families of SQL command, and the question is really whether you know which ones you can undo.

DDL — structure: CREATE, ALTER, DROP, TRUNCATE. Auto-commits in MySQL, so there is nothing to roll back.
DML — data: INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE, SELECT. Can be rolled back inside a transaction.
DCL — permissions: GRANT, REVOKE.
TCL — transactions: COMMIT, ROLLBACK, SAVEPOINT.

The practical point: TRUNCATE is DDL and DELETE is DML, which is exactly why one is recoverable and the other is not.
Q30
Name the constraints and say what each one guarantees.
Answer
NOT NULL — the column must have a value.
UNIQUE — no two rows share this value, but NULLs are allowed and several NULLs do not count as duplicates of each other.
PRIMARY KEY — unique and not null, one per table, and it creates an index.
FOREIGN KEY — the value must exist in the referenced table.
CHECK — an arbitrary condition, such as salary > 0.
DEFAULT — a value used when none is supplied.

Why an analyst should care about constraints they will never write: they are what makes a join trustworthy. Every data-quality check you run — orphan keys, duplicates, impossible values — is checking for something a constraint would have prevented at the source.
Q31
Primary key, candidate key, composite key, alternate key, super key — define them.
Answer
They sound like trivia and they are one idea viewed from different angles.

Super key — any set of columns that uniquely identifies a row, including ones with redundant columns bolted on.
Candidate key — a super key with nothing spare: remove any column and it stops being unique. A table can have several.
Primary key — the candidate key you chose.
Alternate key — the candidate keys you did not choose.
Composite key — a key made of more than one column.

A concrete example lands it: in the order lines table, (order_id, prod_id) is a composite primary key — neither column is unique alone, and together they are.
Q32
Walk me through 1NF, 2NF and 3NF with an example.
Answer
Do it on one table rather than reciting definitions.

Start with an orders table holding order_id, products ('A, B'), cust_name, cust_city, prod_id, prod_price.

1NF — no repeating groups; every cell holds one value. The comma-separated product list has to become one row per product.
2NF — 1NF, plus no non-key column depends on only part of a composite key. The key is now (order_id, prod_id), and cust_name depends on order_id alone — so customer details move to their own table.
3NF — 2NF, plus no non-key column depends on another non-key column. cust_city depends on the customer, not on the order, so it belongs in the customer table.

BCNF is a stricter 3NF for the unusual case where a column that is part of a candidate key depends on something else. The one-liner for the whole set: every fact stored once, in the table it belongs to.
Q33
What do the cardinalities on an ER diagram mean, and how do you handle many-to-many?
Answer
Cardinality is how many of this relate to how many of that: one-to-one, one-to-many, many-to-many. Optionality is the separate question of whether zero is allowed — an employee may have no manager.

One-to-many is implemented by putting the foreign key on the many side. An employee carries a dept_id; the department does not carry a list of employees.

Many-to-many cannot be implemented directly. You need a third table — a junction, bridge or associative table — holding a foreign key to each side, with the pair as its composite primary key. Orders and products is exactly this: one order has many products, one product is on many orders, and order_items is the bridge. Any extra fact about the pairing, such as quantity or the price charged, naturally lives in that bridge table.
Q34
What is a transaction, and what does ACID mean?beyond the class syllabus
Answer
A transaction is a group of statements that must all succeed or all fail. Moving money between accounts is the standard example: two updates, and one of them happening alone is worse than neither.

Atomicity — all or nothing.
Consistency — the database moves from one valid state to another; constraints hold at the end.
Isolation — concurrent transactions do not see each other's half-finished work.
Durability — once committed, it survives a crash.

Why it matters to an analyst: START TRANSACTION before a hand-written UPDATE or DELETE gives you a ROLLBACK, and it is the difference between a mistake and an incident.
START TRANSACTION;
UPDATE employees SET salary = salary * 1.10 WHERE dept_id = 4;
SELECT COUNT(*) FROM employees WHERE dept_id = 4;  -- check first
COMMIT;   -- or ROLLBACK;

Functions and conditionals

Q35
IFNULL, COALESCE and NULLIF — what does each do?
Answer
IFNULL(a, b) — returns b if a is NULL. Exactly two arguments, MySQL-specific.
COALESCE(a, b, c, ...) — returns the first non-NULL argument. Any number of arguments, and it is standard SQL, so prefer it.
NULLIF(a, b) — returns NULL if the two are equal, otherwise a. It looks backwards until you see its real use: NULLIF(denominator, 0) turns a divide-by-zero error into a NULL, which is the standard way to make a rate calculation safe.

The caution to add: replacing a NULL with 0 changes your averages. COALESCE(salary, 0) in an AVG drags the mean down. Do it for display, not inside a statistic.
COALESCE(e.manager_name, 'No manager')
ROUND(revenue / NULLIF(prev_month, 0), 2)
Q36
CASE or IF — which do you use?
Answer
CASE, almost always. It is standard SQL so it works everywhere, it handles any number of branches, and it reads top to bottom like the business rule it encodes. IF(condition, a, b) is MySQL-only and limited to two outcomes; nesting it three deep is unreadable.

Two things about CASE worth knowing: it stops at the first branch that matches, so order your conditions from most specific to least and you rarely need compound conditions. And without an ELSE, unmatched rows get NULL — which is where a silently empty bucket comes from, so write the ELSE.

CASE is also the only way to pivot in MySQL, and it works inside SUM, ORDER BY and GROUP BY, not just SELECT.
SELECT CASE WHEN salary IS NULL   THEN 'not recorded'
            WHEN salary < 60000  THEN 'band 1'
            WHEN salary < 100000 THEN 'band 2'
            ELSE 'band 3'
       END AS band
FROM employees;
Q37
Which string functions do you actually use?
Answer
The short list that covers most real cleaning work:

TRIM / LTRIM / RTRIM — stray spaces, and the first thing to run because a trailing space defeats every join and comparison after it.
UPPER / LOWER — for case-insensitive comparison.
CONCAT and CONCAT_WS — joining, the second one skipping NULLs.
SUBSTRING, LEFT, RIGHT, SUBSTRING_INDEX — pulling parts out.
REPLACE — fixing known variants.
LENGTH and CHAR_LENGTH — finding malformed values; the two differ on multi-byte characters, which matters for Indian-language text.
LPAD — restoring leading zeros that a spreadsheet ate.

The trap to mention: in MySQL CONCAT returns NULL if any argument is NULL, so one missing middle name blanks the whole built string.
Q38
Which date functions do you use, and what is the trap with them?
Answer
CURDATE() and NOW() for today; DATEDIFF(a, b) for days between, later date first; TIMESTAMPDIFF(unit, a, b) for completed months or years; DATE_ADD / DATE_SUB with INTERVAL for shifting; DATE_FORMAT for display; YEAR, MONTH, DAYNAME for parts; LAST_DAY for month ends; STR_TO_DATE for parsing text that arrived in the wrong shape.

Three traps. Wrapping the column in a function kills the index — prefer a range on the bare column. DATEDIFF / 365 is not age, because of leap years and part-years; use TIMESTAMPDIFF(YEAR, ...). And formatted dates sort alphabetically, so group by the sortable form and format only for display.
WHERE hire_date >= '2023-01-01' AND hire_date < '2024-01-01'   -- index-friendly
TIMESTAMPDIFF(YEAR, hire_date, CURDATE())                       -- real tenure
DATE_ADD(order_date, INTERVAL 30 DAY)                           -- due date
Q39
What can you do with REGEXP that LIKE cannot?
Answer
LIKE has two wildcards: % for any run of characters and _ for exactly one. That is all. It cannot express 'one of these letters', 'two or more digits', 'anchored to the end' or 'this pattern repeated'.

REGEXP (synonym RLIKE) gives you the rest:
^ and $ anchors, [AEIOU] character classes, [^...] negation, . any character, * + ? repetition, {2,4} counted repetition, | alternatives, () grouping.

Where it earns its keep for an analyst is validation — finding the phone numbers that are not ten digits, the emails without an @, the pin codes with letters in them. The cost is that it cannot use an index, so filter the rows down first where you can.
WHERE emp_name  REGEXP '^[AEIOU]'          -- starts with a vowel
WHERE phone     REGEXP '^[0-9]{10}$'      -- exactly ten digits
WHERE email NOT REGEXP '^[^@]+@[^@]+\\.[a-z]{2,}$'   -- malformed

Performance and habits

Q40
What is the difference between a clustered and a non-clustered index?
Answer
A clustered index is the table — the rows are physically stored in that order. So there can only be one, and in MySQL's InnoDB it is the primary key whether you asked for it or not.

A non-clustered (secondary) index is a separate structure holding the indexed column plus a pointer back to the row. You can have many. The cost is the extra hop to fetch the rest of the row, which is why a covering index — one that contains every column the query needs — can be dramatically faster.

The consequence worth stating: range scans on the clustered key are very fast because the rows are already adjacent on disk, which is a real argument for a sensible primary key rather than a random one.
Q41
A query that used to take two seconds now takes four minutes. How do you investigate?
Answer
A method question, so give a method rather than a list of tricks.

1. Read the plan. EXPLAIN is the first command, every time. You are looking for a full table scan where you expected an index lookup, and for a rows estimate wildly larger than the result.
2. Ask what changed. Data volume, a new index dropped, statistics gone stale, someone else's load on the box. 'Nothing changed' is almost never true.
3. Check the usual causes — a function wrapped round an indexed column, an implicit type conversion in the join, SELECT * pulling columns nobody needs, a join that fans out and a DISTINCT papering over it, OR across two columns.
4. Fix the cheapest thing first and measure after each change, one at a time.

Candidates who say 'add an index' as the whole answer have not been on the other side of it: indexes slow every write and the wrong one does nothing.
EXPLAIN SELECT ... ;          -- read this before changing anything
Q42
Why is SELECT * considered bad practice?
Answer
Four reasons, and the last one is the one people miss.

It moves columns you do not need across the network, which on a wide table is most of the cost. It defeats covering indexes, so the database has to visit the table. It makes the query unreadable — nobody can tell what the result is meant to contain. And it is fragile: add a column upstream and your downstream code, which was addressing columns by position or building a DataFrame of an expected shape, breaks or quietly changes meaning.

Where it is fine: exploring interactively, and SELECT COUNT(*), which does not read the columns at all. The habit to describe is SELECT * while you are looking, named columns in anything you save.
Q43
What is SQL injection, and why should an analyst know about it?
Answer
Building a query by pasting user input into a string, so that input can change the query's meaning. The classic is a login form where entering ' OR '1'='1 makes the WHERE clause always true.

The fix is parameterised queries — pass values separately and let the driver handle them — never string formatting, and never manual quote-escaping.

Why an analyst is asked: the moment you write a Python script that builds SQL from a variable, you are writing the vulnerable pattern. Even with trusted input it breaks on a name containing an apostrophe, so the safe version is also the one that works.
# wrong
cur.execute("SELECT * FROM employees WHERE city = '" + city + "'")

# right
cur.execute("SELECT * FROM employees WHERE city = %s", (city,))
Q44
What is a temporary table, and when would you use one over a subquery?
Answer
A table that exists for your session only and disappears when you disconnect. In MySQL, CREATE TEMPORARY TABLE.

Reach for one when an intermediate result is used more than once and is expensive to compute — a subquery or a CTE is re-evaluated at each reference, while a temporary table is computed once and can be indexed. It also makes a long multi-step transformation readable and debuggable, because you can select from each step and check it.

Use a subquery or CTE instead when the result is used once and the readability win is the whole point. The decision is: computed once and reused, or expressed once and read?

Filtering, sorting and limits

Q01
List employees in Bengaluru earning more than ₹70,000, highest paid first.
Answer

SELECT emp_name, designation, salary

Q02
Find everyone hired in 2023.
Answer
Both forms below return the same four rows, but they are not equal in an interview.

YEAR(hire_date) = 2023 wraps the column in a function, so the database cannot use an index on hire_date — it has to compute YEAR() for every row. A range on the bare column can use the index. On 22 rows it makes no difference; on 22 million it is the whole answer. Say the range version is what you would ship and the YEAR() version is what you would write while exploring.
-- fine while exploring, but the function blocks the index

The single most-asked SQL question there is. Three answers, and the best candidates give more than one.

2. DISTINCT then OFFSET — fixes the tie.

  SELECT salary, DENSE_RANK() OVER (ORDER BY salary DESC) AS r
  FROM employees
) t WHERE r = 2;
-- 2. the second distinct SALARY
SELECT DISTINCT salary
FROM employees
ORDER BY salary DESC
LIMIT 1 OFFSET 1;

UNION removes duplicate rows; UNION ALL keeps everything. Deduplicating costs a sort or a hash, so UNION ALL is faster and is what you want whenever you already know the halves cannot overlap.

Count that as an order and they look active; count only completed orders and they are your one genuinely lapsed account. Same data, opposite conclusion — which is the lesson.
SELECT c.cust_id, c.cust_name, c.segment
FROM customers c
WHERE EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM orders o
              WHERE o.cust_id = c.cust_id AND o.status = 'completed'
                AND o.order_date >= '2024-01-01' AND o.order_date < '2025-01-01')
  AND NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM orders o
              WHERE o.cust_id = c.cust_id AND o.status = 'completed'
                AND o.order_date >= '2025-01-01')
ORDER BY c.cust_id;

Q33

Answer
Ask this once and you never have to ask what a window function is.

ROW_NUMBER — 1, 2, 3, 4 always. Ties are broken arbitrarily, so it never repeats and never skips.
RANK — ties share a number, then it skips: 1, 2, 2, 4.
DENSE_RANK — ties share a number and it does not skip: 1, 2, 2, 3.

PARTITION BY restarts the numbering for each department; without it you would rank across the whole company. And unlike GROUP BY, the window keeps every row — that is the whole reason window functions exist.

SELECT a.* FROM staging a
JOIN staging b ON a.email = b.email AND a.amount = b.amount AND a.id > b.id;

-- then delete
DELETE a FROM staging a
JOIN staging b
  ON a.email = b.email AND a.amount = b.amount
 AND a.id > b.id;

Q54
Write a stored procedure that returns the employees of one department.
Answer
A stored procedure is a named block of SQL saved in the database and called with CALL. It takes parameters (IN, OUT, INOUT), can hold several statements, loops and error handling, and runs on the server so the data never travels.

How it differs from the two things it gets confused with: a view takes no parameters and is just a query you can select from; a function returns a single value and can be used inside an expression, while a procedure returns result sets and is invoked on its own. DELIMITER has to be changed because the body contains semicolons that MySQL would otherwise treat as the end of the statement.
DELIMITER $$

CREATE PROCEDURE get_dept_employees(IN p_dept VARCHAR(50))
BEGIN
  SELECT e.emp_name, e.designation, e.salary
  FROM employees e
  JOIN departments d ON e.dept_id = d.dept_id
  WHERE d.dept_name = p_dept
  ORDER BY e.salary DESC;
END$$

DELIMITER ;

CALL get_dept_employees('Analytics');
Q55
Write a trigger that records every salary change.
Answer
A trigger is SQL that fires automatically on an INSERT, UPDATE or DELETE — nobody calls it. Inside the body, OLD holds the row as it was and NEW holds the row as it will be, so an audit trail is a two-line insert.

The balanced answer includes the cost: triggers run inside your transaction, so they slow every write; they are invisible to anyone reading the application code, which makes surprises hard to debug; and a trigger that writes to a table with its own trigger can cascade. Use them for audit and integrity, not for business logic.
CREATE TABLE salary_audit (
  emp_id     INT,
  old_salary INT,
  new_salary INT,
  changed_at DATETIME
);

CREATE TRIGGER trg_salary_change
AFTER UPDATE ON employees
FOR EACH ROW
BEGIN
  IF OLD.salary <> NEW.salary THEN
    INSERT INTO salary_audit
    VALUES (OLD.emp_id, OLD.salary, NEW.salary, NOW());
  END IF;
END;

The objects

Q01
What is the difference between a Series and a DataFrame?
Answer
A Series is one labelled column — one dimension, one dtype, plus an index. A DataFrame is a two-dimensional table: a set of Series sharing one index, each able to hold a different dtype.

Selecting a single column with df['col'] returns a Series; df[['col']] returns a one-column DataFrame.
df['salary']      # Series
df[['salary']]    # DataFrame
Q02
What is the index, and why does it matter?
Answer
The row labels. Pandas adds 0,1,2 if you do not supply one. It matters for three reasons: loc works on it, operations between objects align on it, and groupby returns results indexed by the group.

Index alignment is the one that catches people — adding two Series matches on label, not position, so mismatched indexes give NaN rather than an error.
Q03
How do you read a file into pandas?
Answer
pd.read_csv() for CSV, pd.read_excel() for a workbook, pd.read_sql() for a query. Useful arguments to mention: nrows to peek at a huge file, usecols to load only what you need, parse_dates so date columns arrive as dates rather than text, and na_values for the file's own idea of missing.

Selecting

Q04
What is the difference between loc and iloc?
Answer
loc selects by label; iloc selects by integer position.

The detail that separates answers: loc slicing includes the stop value, iloc excludes it. df.loc[0:3] returns four rows; df.iloc[0:3] returns three. On a default 0,1,2 index they look identical, which is exactly why the bug is hard to find.
df.loc[0:3]     # 4 rows - INCLUSIVE
df.iloc[0:3]    # 3 rows - exclusive
df.loc[0:2, ['name','salary']]
Q05
What are at and iat?
Answer
Single-cell accessors. at takes a row label and a column name; iat takes both as positions. They are faster than loc/iloc for one value because they skip the general machinery.

Both can be assigned to, which is the tidy way to set one cell: df.at[0,'name'] = 'David'.
Q06
How do you filter rows on more than one condition?
Answer
With & for and, | for or, and brackets around each condition.

The word and raises ValueError: The truth value of a Series is ambiguous, because and expects one True or False and you handed it a whole column. The brackets are required because & binds tighter than the comparison operators.
df[(df['dept'] == 'Tech') & (df['salary'] > 50000)]
Q07
What does the SettingWithCopyWarning mean?
Answer
Pandas is telling you it cannot be sure whether you are modifying the original DataFrame or a temporary copy, so your assignment may silently do nothing.

It is caused by chained indexing — two sets of brackets in a row. The fix is a single loc call, or an explicit .copy() if you genuinely wanted a separate object.
df[df['dept']=='Tech']['salary'] = 0     # warns, may do nothing
df.loc[df['dept']=='Tech', 'salary'] = 0  # correct

Cleaning

Q08
How do you find and handle missing values?
Answer
df.isnull().sum() gives the count per column — it works because True counts as 1. Then choose:

dropna() removes rows, and by default removes a row if any column is blank, which is more aggressive than people expect. subset= restricts it to the columns you care about.
fillna() keeps the row and substitutes a value — usually the median for a skewed numeric column, the mode or a literal 'Unknown' for a category.

There is no correct answer, only a declared one. Say which you chose and why.
df.isnull().sum()
df = df.dropna(subset=['salary'])
df['age'] = df['age'].fillna(df['age'].median())
Q09
Why did my dropna / fillna not change anything?
Answer
Because almost every pandas method returns a new object and leaves the original alone. You have to assign the result back.

df.dropna() computes and discards; df = df.dropna() keeps it. The same is true of sort_values, drop_duplicates, rename and astype.
Q10
How do you remove duplicates?
Answer
df.duplicated().sum() counts them, df.drop_duplicates() removes them. subset= limits the comparison to certain columns, and keep='first'|'last'|False controls which copy survives — keep=False drops every copy, which is occasionally what you want.
Q11
How do you change a column's type, and when does it fail?
Answer
astype(). It fails on a numeric cast when the column contains NaN — IntCastingNaNError: cannot convert non-finite values to integer — because there is no integer for 'missing'.

So the order is: handle the blanks first, convert second. pd.to_numeric(..., errors='coerce') is the forgiving alternative, turning anything unparseable into NaN.

Grouping and reshaping

Q12
Explain groupby.
Answer
Split, apply, combine. Split the rows into groups by a column, apply a function to each group, combine the results into one table.

The function must reduce each group to a single value, which is why a bare df.groupby('dept') prints nothing — it has grouped, but there is no instruction for what to show.
df.groupby('department')['salary'].mean()
df.groupby('department')['salary'].agg(['count','mean','max'])
df.groupby(['department','city'])['salary'].mean()
Q13
Why does groupby count differ from value_counts?
Answer
value_counts() and size() count rows. count inside an aggregation counts non-null values in the column being aggregated.

So a department with 49 people but one missing salary shows 49 from value_counts and 48 from the aggregation. The gap is your missing data, and groupby skips it without warning.
Q14
What is the difference between merge, join and concat?
Answer
merge combines on a shared column or index — it is the SQL join, and takes how='inner'|'left'|'right'|'outer'.
join is a convenience method that merges on the index by default.
concat glues objects together by position, adding rows (axis=0) or columns (axis=1). It matches nothing.

Shorthand: merge matches, concat stacks.
pd.merge(a, b, on='emp_id', how='left')
pd.concat([a, b])              # more rows
pd.concat([a, b], axis=1)      # more columns
Q15
Your merge returned fewer rows than you started with. What happened?
Answer
The default is an inner join, so rows with no match on the other side were dropped — silently, with no warning.

Always print len() before and after. If you need to keep everything on the left, use how='left'; the unmatched rows then arrive with NaN, which is at least visible. indicator=True adds a column showing which side each row came from, which is the fastest way to diagnose it.
Q16
What is a pivot_table, and how does it differ from groupby?
Answer
pivot_table is groupby in two directions at once — one field down the rows, another across the columns, with an aggregation in the middle. It also takes margins=True for totals and fill_value for empty cells.

It is the direct equivalent of an Excel pivot table, which makes it a good thing to mention if the role is Excel-heavy.
df.pivot_table(index='department', columns='rating',
               values='salary', aggfunc='mean')
Q17
What does apply do, and when should you avoid it?
Answer
apply runs your own function down a column or across rows. It is the escape hatch when no built-in exists.

Avoid it when a vectorised alternative exists, because apply loops in Python and is far slower on large frames. df['a'] * 2 beats df['a'].apply(lambda x: x*2) by a wide margin.

Everyday questions

Q18
How do you get a quick feel for a new dataset?
Answer
A fixed sequence, and saying it in order is itself the answer:

df.shape how big · df.head() what it looks like · df.info() types and non-null counts · df.describe() the numeric spread · df.isnull().sum() what is missing · df['col'].value_counts() for the categories.

info() tells you what is missing; describe() tells you what is strange.
Q19
What does describe() actually show, and what does it leave out?
Answer
Count, mean, standard deviation, min, the three quartiles and max — for numeric columns only. Text columns are excluded unless you pass include='all', which then adds unique, top and freq.

The useful read is comparing mean with the 50% row: a large gap means the column is skewed and the mean is being pulled by outliers.
Q20
How would you find outliers in a column?
Answer
Two standard approaches. IQR: anything below Q1 − 1.5×IQR or above Q3 + 1.5×IQR. Z-score: anything more than about 3 standard deviations from the mean.

IQR is the safer default because the mean and standard deviation are themselves distorted by the outliers you are hunting. And the judgement matters more than the method: an impossible value is a data error, an extreme but real value is a finding.
q1, q3 = df['salary'].quantile([.25,.75])
iqr = q3 - q1
df[(df['salary'] < q1-1.5*iqr) | (df['salary'] > q3+1.5*iqr)]
Q21
How do you create a new column from existing ones?
Answer
Assign to a new name: df["total"] = df["qty"] * df["price"]. That is vectorised — the whole column at once — and it is both the fastest and the clearest form. Reaching for apply or a loop to do arithmetic is the most common sign of someone who learned pandas as a syntax rather than as a way of working.

For conditional columns: np.where(cond, a, b) for two outcomes, np.select([c1, c2], [v1, v2], default=...) for several. Both beat a nested apply by a wide margin.

Two practical notes. assign() returns a new frame, so it chains — useful in a pipeline where you do not want to mutate the input. And if you are assigning into a slice of another frame you will meet SettingWithCopyWarning; the fix is .copy() when you take the slice, not suppressing the warning.
df['bonus']  = df['salary'] * 0.10
df['band']   = np.where(df['salary'] > 60000, 'high', 'low')
df['bucket'] = pd.cut(df['experience'], bins=[0,2,7,100],
                      labels=['Junior','Mid','Senior'])
Q22
How do you work with dates in pandas?
Answer
pd.to_datetime() converts a text column to real dates, after which the .dt accessor gives you the parts: .dt.year, .dt.month, .dt.day_name().

Until you convert, a date column is just text and nothing date-like works. Deriving the weekday is a good example of creating a column that answers a question the raw file could not.

Columns, types and indexes

Q23
How do you rename columns, and what is the safest way?
Answer
df.rename(columns={"old": "new"}) renames by name and leaves the rest alone, which is why it is the safe option. Assigning to df.columns replaces the whole list by position, so one extra column upstream silently shifts every name onto the wrong data — a genuinely dangerous bug because nothing errors.

For bulk cleaning, pass a function: df.rename(columns=str.lower), or a comprehension that strips spaces and replaces them with underscores. Doing that once at read time saves endless bracket-quoting later.
df = df.rename(columns={"Emp Name": "emp_name"})
df.columns = [c.strip().lower().replace(" ", "_") for c in df.columns]
Q24
What does inplace=True do, and should you use it?
Answer
It modifies the object instead of returning a new one. Prefer not to use it.

Three reasons: it does not reliably save memory (pandas often builds a copy internally anyway), it breaks method chaining because it returns None, and df = df.dropna(inplace=True) — a slip people make constantly — sets your DataFrame to None with no error.

It is being deprecated across pandas for exactly these reasons. Assigning the result is clearer and is what the library is moving towards.
df = df.dropna()           # do this
df.dropna(inplace=True)    # not this
Q25
map, apply and applymap — what is the difference?
Answer
map — Series only, element by element. Takes a function, a dict or a Series, which makes it the natural tool for recoding categories.
apply — on a Series it works element-wise; on a DataFrame it works on a whole row or column depending on axis, which is what you want when a calculation needs several columns at once.
applymap — DataFrame, every single cell. Renamed map on DataFrames in pandas 2.1, so mention both names.

The point to land: all three are loops in disguise. If a vectorised expression or np.where can do it, that will be far faster. Use apply when the logic genuinely will not vectorise, not as a first resort.
df["city"].map({"bangalore": "Bengaluru"})        # recode
df.apply(lambda r: r["qty"] * r["price"], axis=1)   # across columns
df["qty"] * df["price"]                             # better - vectorised
Q26
How do you convert a column to a number when the data is dirty?
Answer
astype(int) raises on the first bad value, which is useless on real data. pd.to_numeric(col, errors="coerce") turns anything unparseable into NaN so the whole column converts, and then you can count and inspect the failures.

That two-step is the whole answer: coerce, then look at what became NaN. Those rows are your data-quality report — currency symbols, thousands separators, 'N/A', a stray footnote. Fix the ones you can with str.replace first and report the rest.

Note that introducing NaN forces the column to float64, because NumPy integers cannot hold a missing value. The nullable Int64 dtype can, if you need integers with gaps.
s = pd.Series(["1200", "1,300", "Rs 1400", "N/A"])
num = pd.to_numeric(s.str.replace(r"[^0-9.]", "", regex=True), errors="coerce")
print(num.isna().sum(), "values could not be parsed")
Q27
What is a MultiIndex, how do you get one by accident, and how do you flatten it?
Answer
An index with more than one level. You get one by accident two ways: grouping by two columns, or aggregating with a list of functions — which makes the columns a MultiIndex.

It is powerful and almost always more trouble than it is worth mid-analysis, because every later .loc and merge has to know about it. Flatten it immediately: reset_index() turns index levels back into columns, and a list comprehension joins multi-level column names.

The way to avoid it entirely is named aggregation — agg(total=("x", "sum")) — which produces flat column names in the first place.
g = df.groupby(["city", "dept"]).agg({"salary": ["mean", "max"]})
g.columns = ["_".join(c) for c in g.columns]
g = g.reset_index()
Q28
What is the categorical dtype and why would you use it?
Answer
It stores the column as integer codes plus a lookup of the distinct values. For a text column with few distinct values and many rows, the memory saving is large — often ten-fold.

Two further benefits beyond memory: operations like groupby get faster, and you can give the categories a meaningful order. That is how you make 'Low, Medium, High' sort correctly instead of alphabetically, and it is the clean fix for the chart whose axis is in the wrong order.

The gotcha: once a column is categorical, assigning a value that is not already a category fails. Add it with add_categories first.
df["segment"] = df["segment"].astype("category")
df["band"] = pd.Categorical(df["band"],
                             categories=["Low", "Medium", "High"], ordered=True)

Q29
How do you bin a numeric column into groups?
Answer
pd.cut cuts at values you choose — equal-width bins, or edges that mean something to the business. pd.qcut cuts at quantiles, so each bin holds roughly the same number of rows.

The choice is the answer. Salary bands for a report want cut, because '60k to 100k' is a phrase people understand. 'Top 20% of customers by spend' wants qcut, because the definition is about rank, not value.

Two details: pass labels= or you get interval objects that are awkward to read, and cut is right-closed by default, so a value exactly on an edge goes into the lower bin. Say which convention you used when the boundary matters.
pd.cut(df["salary"], bins=[0, 60000, 100000, 150000, np.inf],
       labels=["under 60k", "60-100k", "100-150k", "150k+"])
pd.qcut(df["salary"], q=4, labels=["Q1", "Q2", "Q3", "Q4"])
Q30
How do you calculate a rolling average, and what do you watch for?
Answer
df["x"].rolling(7).mean() — a seven-period moving average, which is the standard way to take weekday noise out of a daily series.

Three things to watch. The first six values are NaN, because a seven-day window needs seven days; min_periods relaxes that if you accept a less stable start. The window is trailing by default, so the value is the average of the last seven days, not centred on the day — use center=True if you mean the latter, and never mix the two in one chart. And the data must be sorted by date, with no missing days, or '7' is not seven days; rolling("7D") on a datetime index handles gaps correctly.
df = df.sort_values("date")
df["ma7"] = df["revenue"].rolling(7, min_periods=1).mean()
Q31
How do you compare each row with the previous period?
Answer
shift(1) moves the column down one row, which lines the previous value up beside the current one — it is pandas' LAG. diff() is the difference, and pct_change() the proportional change.

For period-over-period within groups, do it inside a groupby so one customer's history does not leak into the next: df.groupby("cust_id")["amount"].shift(1).

Two cautions: the data must be sorted, and pct_change returns inf when the previous value is zero rather than raising, so a growth column full of inf means you divided by a zero month.
df = df.sort_values(["cust_id", "date"])
df["prev"]   = df.groupby("cust_id")["amount"].shift(1)
df["growth"] = df.groupby("cust_id")["amount"].pct_change()
Q32
Why is iterrows slow, and what should you use instead?
Answer
iterrows builds a new Series object for every row, so you pay Python object creation per row and lose the dtype in the process — an integer column comes back as whatever type fits the whole row. On a hundred thousand rows it is orders of magnitude slower than the vectorised equivalent.

In order of preference: a vectorised expression; np.where or np.select for conditional logic; apply(axis=1) if the logic truly needs the whole row; itertuples() if you really must iterate, since it is several times faster and preserves types.

The honest caveat: for a few hundred rows it does not matter and clarity wins. The answer the interviewer wants is that you know the cost and choose deliberately.
# slow
for i, row in df.iterrows():
    df.loc[i, "total"] = row["qty"] * row["price"]

# fast
df["total"] = df["qty"] * df["price"]
Q33
You are given a 5 GB CSV and 8 GB of RAM. What do you do?
Answer
A practical question, and there is a sequence.

1. Read less. usecols= to take only the columns you need, which is often the whole fix. nrows=1000 first to see what you are dealing with.
2. Read it smaller. Pass explicit dtype= — int32 instead of int64, category for low-cardinality text. This commonly halves the footprint or better.
3. Read it in pieces. chunksize= gives an iterator; aggregate each chunk and combine the results. Works whenever the operation is a sum, count or groupby.
4. Change the format. Convert once to Parquet — columnar, compressed, typed, and far faster to read the columns you want.
5. Change the tool. Push the aggregation into SQL, or use Polars or DuckDB, which handle larger-than-memory data directly.

The instinct being tested is whether you reach for a bigger machine first or ask what you actually need first.
df = pd.read_csv(f, usecols=["date", "cust_id", "amount"],
                 dtype={"cust_id": "int32"}, parse_dates=["date"])

total = sum(c.groupby("cust_id")["amount"].sum()
            for c in pd.read_csv(f, chunksize=500_000))
Q34
What does value_counts give you, and which arguments matter?
Answer
A frequency table of a Series, sorted by count, descending. The fastest way to see what is in a column.

The arguments that matter:
dropna=False — include the missing values. This should almost be the default habit, because the whole point of looking at a column is to find out what is wrong with it, and by default the NaNs are hidden from you.
normalize=True — proportions instead of counts, which is usually the more useful number.
bins= — for a numeric column, buckets it first.

Contrast with nunique(), which gives you just the count of distinct values, and unique(), which gives the values themselves in order of appearance.
df["city"].value_counts(dropna=False)
df["city"].value_counts(normalize=True).round(3)
Q35
How do you merge when the key columns have different names, or the key is the index?
Answer
left_on= and right_on= for differently named columns; left_index=True / right_index=True when the key is an index. Mixing them is allowed: left_on="dept_id", right_index=True.

Two things worth volunteering. Use suffixes= to control what happens to the columns that exist on both sides, or you get the unhelpful _x and _y and have to work out which was which later.

And use validate=. validate="one_to_one" or "one_to_many" makes pandas raise if the keys are not as unique as you assumed. That one argument turns the most common silent merge bug into an exception, and almost nobody uses it.
m = emp.merge(dept, left_on="dept_id", right_index=True,
              how="left", suffixes=("", "_dept"), validate="many_to_one")

How do you write results out so that the next person can use them?

Beyond that: to_excel with an ExcelWriter for several sheets in one workbook, which is what a stakeholder actually wants. float_format="%.2f" so money does not arrive with fifteen decimal places. to_parquet when the consumer is another script, because it keeps the dtypes and loads far faster.

And the non-technical half: a file called final_v2_updated.xlsx is not a deliverable. Date the filename and say in the sheet what the numbers cover.

Q39
Explain groupby end to end. What is actually happening?long answer
Answer
Groupby is split, apply, combine.

Split — Pandas looks at the column you named and divides the rows into groups, one per distinct value. Apply — it runs your aggregation on each group separately. Combine — it assembles the results into a new object, with the grouping values as the index.

Two things to say about the output. First, the group keys become the index, so you often want .reset_index() to turn them back into a normal column. Second, groupby drops NaN keys by default — rows whose grouping value is missing vanish silently, and your group totals will not add up to the row count. Pass dropna=False if that matters.

.agg() is how you get several statistics at once, and it accepts a dictionary mapping columns to functions.

Q42
What is SettingWithCopyWarning, and what causes it?long answer
Answer
It is Pandas warning you that you may be writing to a copy, so your change may go nowhere.

It happens with chained indexing — two square-bracket operations in a row, like df[df.dept == 'Tech']['salary'] = 0. The first bracket produces a new object, and Pandas cannot promise whether it is a view onto the original or an independent copy. You then assign into that temporary thing, and the original may be untouched.

The fix is to do it in one step with .loc, giving the row condition and the column together, so Pandas knows you mean the original frame.

The related case is working on a filtered subset. If you intend to keep it, say .copy() explicitly — then it is yours, the warning disappears, and your intent is obvious to whoever reads the code.

Explain apply against vectorised operations. When is each one right?long answer
Answer
A vectorised operation pushes the whole column down into NumPy and runs a compiled loop — df['salary'] * 1.1. apply runs your Python function once per row or per value, back in the interpreter. On a large frame the vectorised version is commonly tens of times faster.

Q27
Demonstrate broadcasting, then make it fail.
Answer
Broadcasting stretches a smaller array across a larger one without copying it. The rule, read right to left: two dimensions are compatible if they are equal, or if one of them is 1.

a = np.arange(1, 13).reshape(3, 4)
print(a + np.array([10, 20, 30, 40]))
try:
    a + np.array([1, 2, 3])
except ValueError as e:
    print("\nfails:", e)
print("\nwith reshape(3,1):")
print(a + np.array([1, 2, 3]).reshape(3, 1))

Show the difference between a view and a copy, and why it matters.

a = np.arange(1, 7)

Data types and mutability

Q01
What is the difference between a list and a tuple?
Answer
A list is mutable and uses square brackets; a tuple is immutable and uses round ones.

Beyond the definition, the two points that show understanding: a tuple can be a dictionary key and a list cannot, because keys must be hashable; and a tuple is slightly faster and smaller, which matters when you are holding millions of them.

Use a tuple when the collection should not change — a coordinate, a database row, a fixed set of options.
t = (10, 20, 30)
t[0] = 99          # TypeError: 'tuple' object does not support item assignment
Q02
Which Python types are mutable and which are not?
Answer
Mutable: list, dict, set.
Immutable: int, float, str, bool, tuple, frozenset.

The consequence worth stating: an immutable object cannot be changed in place, so every 'change' creates a new object. That is why repeated string concatenation in a loop is slow and ''.join(...) is preferred.
Q03
What is the difference between a list and a set?
Answer
A list keeps order and allows duplicates; a set does neither. A set also gives you membership testing in roughly constant time because it is hash-based, where a list has to scan.

The everyday use is deduplication: set(my_list). The catch is that you lose the original order and cannot index into it.
entries = ['HR','Tech','HR','Sales']
print(set(entries))        # {'HR','Sales','Tech'} - order not guaranteed
print(len(set(entries)))   # 3
Q04
What is the difference between is and == ?
Answer
== compares values; is compares identity — whether they are the same object in memory.

Two lists with the same contents are == but not is. Use is only for singletons: x is None, x is True.
a = [1,2,3]; b = [1,2,3]
print(a == b)   # True
print(a is b)   # False
Q05
What is the difference between a shallow copy and a deep copy?
Answer
A shallow copy makes a new outer object but the inner objects are shared references. A deep copy recursively copies everything.

It only matters when the structure is nested — a list of lists, a dict of dicts. Changing an inner list through a shallow copy changes the original too, which is a classic source of bugs.
import copy
a = [[1,2],[3,4]]
b = a.copy()              # shallow
c = copy.deepcopy(a)      # deep
b[0][0] = 99
print(a[0][0])            # 99  - the original changed
print(c[0][0])            # 1   - untouched

Functions

Q06
What is the difference between a parameter and an argument?
Answer
The parameter is the name in the function definition. The argument is the value you pass when you call it. A small distinction, but interviewers use it to check whether terms are being used precisely.
Q07
What are *args and **kwargs?
Answer
*args collects any number of positional arguments into a tuple. **kwargs collects any number of keyword arguments into a dictionary.

They let a function accept a variable number of inputs. The order in a signature is fixed: positional, then default, then *args, then **kwargs.
def report(name, *marks, **info):
    print(name, sum(marks), info)

report('Rahul', 45, 72, 88, city='Chennai')
# Rahul 205 {'city': 'Chennai'}
Q08
What is a lambda function and when would you use one?
Answer
An anonymous single-expression function. Useful where a function is needed briefly and naming it would add nothing — as the key to sorted, or inside map and filter.

It cannot contain statements, loops or multiple expressions. If you find yourself wanting those, write a proper function.
staff = [('Rahul', 47764), ('Priya', 62990)]
print(sorted(staff, key=lambda x: x[1], reverse=True))
Q09
Explain map, filter and reduce.
Answer
map applies a function to every item and returns the same number of items.
filter keeps the items where the function is true, so you get fewer.
reduce collapses the whole sequence to one value, two at a time; it has to be imported from functools.

In modern Python a list comprehension is usually preferred to map and filter for readability.
from functools import reduce
m = [45, 72, 88, 61]
print(list(map(lambda x: x+5, m)))         # same count
print(list(filter(lambda x: x>60, m)))     # fewer
print(reduce(lambda a,b: a+b, m))          # one
Q10
What is recursion, and what must every recursive function have?
Answer
A function that calls itself. It must have a base case — a condition that stops the recursion — otherwise it runs until Python raises RecursionError.

Recursion is natural for problems that contain a smaller version of themselves: factorial, Fibonacci, walking a folder tree. It costs stack memory, so an iterative version is often preferred for deep cases.
def fact(n):
    if n <= 1:          # base case
        return 1
    return n * fact(n-1)
Q11
What is the difference between print and return?
Answer
print displays something to the screen; return hands a value back to the caller so it can be stored or used. A function with no return statement returns None.

This catches a surprising number of candidates: a function that prints cannot be used in a calculation.

Comprehensions and idioms

Q12
What is a list comprehension?
Answer
A one-line way to build a list from an iterable, optionally with a filter. It is both shorter and generally faster than an append loop because the list is sized more efficiently.

Note the two positions of if: at the end it filters; at the front, with else, it chooses a value for every item.
[n*n for n in range(1,6)]                        # [1,4,9,16,25]
[m for m in marks if m >= 60]                    # filter
['pass' if m >= 60 else 'fail' for m in marks]   # value for every item
Q13
What does enumerate do?
Answer
Gives you the index and the value together while looping, which replaces the for i in range(len(items)) pattern and the items[i] lookups that go with it. Shorter, and it cannot go out of range.

It takes an optional start, which is how you number output from 1 for a human while keeping Python's own indexing at 0.

Worth adding that it returns an iterator of tuples, not a list, so it is lazy and costs nothing on a long sequence — and you can unpack it directly in the for statement, which is the idiomatic form.
for i, d in enumerate(['HR','Tech','Sales'], start=1):
    print(i, d)
Q14
What does zip do?
Answer
Pairs up items from two or more iterables, position by position, returning an iterator of tuples. It is how you walk two lists together, and dict(zip(keys, values)) is the standard way to build a dictionary from two sequences.

The trap: it stops at the shortest input and says nothing. Zipping 2 names with 3 salaries silently gives you 2 pairs and the third salary is gone — exactly the kind of quiet data loss that is impossible to spot downstream. zip(..., strict=True) raises instead, from Python 3.10, and itertools.zip_longest pads with a fill value if you want everything.

The other thing to know is zip(*rows), which transposes — it turns a list of rows into a list of columns, and it is a neat one-liner to have ready.
names = ['Rahul','Priya']
sal   = [47764, 62990, 51200]
print(list(zip(names, sal)))   # 2 pairs - the third salary is dropped
Q15
What is the difference between range and a list?
Answer
range is lazy — it generates numbers on demand and stores only start, stop and step. A list of a million numbers holds a million objects in memory; range(1000000) holds three.

This is the easiest way into a question about generators and memory.
Q16
What is a generator?
Answer
A function that uses yield instead of return, producing values one at a time and remembering where it left off. It does not build the whole result in memory, which is why it matters for large files.

Reading a 10 GB log line by line is the standard example.
def squares(n):
    for i in range(n):
        yield i*i

for s in squares(5):
    print(s, end=' ')

Errors and files

Q17
How does exception handling work in Python?
Answer
try holds the risky code, except handles a failure, else runs only if nothing failed, and finally runs either way — which is where cleanup goes.

The point to make: catch a specific exception. A bare except swallows everything including bugs you wanted to know about.
try:
    v = int(value)
except ValueError:
    print('not a number')
else:
    print('worked:', v)
finally:
    print('always runs')
Q18
Name some common Python exceptions.
Answer
ValueError right type, wrong value (int('abc')). TypeError wrong type altogether ('5' + 5). KeyError dictionary key missing. IndexError list position out of range. ZeroDivisionError. FileNotFoundError. AttributeError the object has no such method, which is what you get from a typo in a method name.
Q19
Why use `with open(...)` instead of open and close?
Answer
Because with closes the file automatically when the block ends, including if an exception is raised inside it. A manual close() is skipped when the code above it fails, and the file stays locked or unflushed.
Q20
What are the file modes?
Answer
r read (default). w write — creates or wipes the file. a append. r+ read and write. Adding b makes it binary.

The one that costs people data is w: opening in write mode empties the file before you write anything.

Object orientation

Q21
What is the difference between a class and an object?
Answer
A class is the blueprint; an object is one instance built from it. One class, many objects, each carrying its own data while sharing the same methods.

The analogy that lands: the class is the form design, each filled-in form is an object. Or in analyst terms, pd.DataFrame is the class and the frame you just loaded is an object — which is also the answer to 'where have you used this?', because every df.groupby() is a method call on an object.

The follow-up to be ready for is where the data lives: attributes set on self belong to the individual object, while a variable defined in the class body is shared by all of them. That is the next question in this section, and it is where the real bugs are.
Q22
What is __init__ and what is self?
Answer
__init__ is the constructor — it runs automatically when an object is created and sets up its starting state. self is the reference to the object being worked on, which is how each instance keeps its own attributes separate.

self is passed automatically; you write it in the definition but not in the call.
class Employee:
    def __init__(self, name, salary):
        self.name = name
        self.salary = salary
Q23
Explain the four pillars of OOP.
Answer
Encapsulation — data and the methods that use it live together, with internal detail marked private.
Inheritance — a class takes the attributes and methods of a parent.
Polymorphism — the same method name behaving differently depending on the class, usually by overriding.
Abstraction — exposing what something does while hiding how.

Give a one-line example of each rather than only the definitions; that is what separates a memorised answer.
Q24
What is method overriding?
Answer
A child class defining a method the parent already has, so the child's version runs instead. super() lets the child call the parent's version first and then add to it, rather than duplicating it.
class Manager(Employee):
    def __init__(self, name, salary, team):
        super().__init__(name, salary)
        self.team = team
Q25
What is the difference between an instance variable and a class variable?
Answer
An instance variable is set on self and belongs to one object. A class variable is defined in the class body and is shared by every instance.

The classic trap is a mutable class variable — a list — which every object then shares and appends to.

Dictionaries and collections

Q26
What is a dictionary, and which methods do you use?
Answer
Key-value pairs with constant-time lookup, which is the reason to use one — finding a value in a list of 100,000 items means scanning it; in a dict it is one step.

Keys must be immutable (hashable), so a string or tuple can be a key and a list cannot. Since Python 3.7 dicts keep insertion order.

The methods worth naming: keys(), values(), items() for iterating; get(key, default) to read without risking a KeyError; setdefault and update; pop. get is the one that matters — reading a missing key with brackets raises, and half of all dictionary bugs are that.
counts.get("mumbai", 0)        # safe
counts["mumbai"]               # KeyError if absent
for k, v in counts.items(): ...
Q27
What is a dictionary comprehension?
Answer
The same idea as a list comprehension, producing a dict. {k: v for ...}, with the colon being the only difference in syntax.

The two everyday uses: inverting a dict, and filtering one. Both read better than the equivalent loop and both are a common quick exercise in an interview, so it is worth having the shape in your fingers.

Note the trap on inverting: if two keys share a value, the inverted dict silently keeps only the last one.
{v: k for k, v in d.items()}                  # invert
{k: v for k, v in d.items() if v > 100}       # filter
{c: len(c) for c in cities}                   # build
Q28
What is the difference between sort() and sorted(), and how do you sort by something other than the value?
Answer
list.sort() sorts in place and returns None. sorted(anything) returns a new list and works on any iterable — a string, a dict, a set, a generator.

So x = my_list.sort() gives you None, which is one of the most common beginner bugs and worth watching for.

Both take key=, a function applied to each element to decide what to sort on. That is how you sort names case-insensitively, sort a list of tuples by the second item, or sort a list of dictionaries by a field. reverse=True for descending.
sorted(names, key=str.lower)
sorted(rows, key=lambda r: r["salary"], reverse=True)
sorted(d.items(), key=lambda kv: kv[1])     # dict by value
Q29
Explain what is wrong with this function.
Answer
The classic mutable default argument trap. The default [] is created once, when the function is defined — not on each call. So every call that omits the argument shares the same list, and it keeps growing.

The fix is None as the default and building the list inside. The same applies to {} and to any other mutable default.

Being able to explain why — that default values are evaluated at definition time — is what the question is really testing.
def add_item(x, basket=[]):      # wrong
    basket.append(x)
    return basket

add_item(2)   # [1, 2]  <- surprise

def add_item(x, basket=None):    # right
    if basket is None:
        basket = []
    basket.append(x)
    return basket
Q30
What does Counter do, and when would you use it over a dict?
Answer
collections.Counter is a dict specialised for counting. It returns 0 instead of a KeyError for a missing key, counts any iterable in one call, and gives you most_common(n) sorted by frequency.

It is the Python answer to value_counts() in pandas and GROUP BY ... COUNT(*) in SQL, and it saves the if key in d dance entirely.

Idioms and scope

Q31
What is the difference between append and extend?
Answer
append adds one element. extend adds each element of an iterable.

a.append([1, 2]) makes the list one longer, and that last element is itself a list. a.extend([1, 2]) makes it two longer. Appending when you meant to extend gives you nested lists, which then fail somewhere else entirely — usually inside pandas.

Also know insert(i, x) for a position and + / +=, where += on a list behaves like extend.
a = [1, 2]
a.append([3, 4])    # [1, 2, [3, 4]]
a = [1, 2]
a.extend([3, 4])    # [1, 2, 3, 4]
Q32
Strings are immutable. What follows from that?
Answer
You cannot change a character in place — s[0] = "X" raises. Every operation that looks like it edits a string actually returns a new one, which is why s.upper() does nothing unless you assign the result. That single misunderstanding accounts for a lot of 'my cleaning code did not work'.

The performance consequence: building a string with += in a loop creates a new string every iteration. Use "".join(parts) instead.

And the upside: because they are immutable, strings can be dictionary keys and are safe to pass around.
s.upper()           # returns a new string
s = s.upper()       # this is what you meant

", ".join(cities)   # not += in a loop
Q33
Explain slicing, including negative indices and steps.
Answer
s[start:stop:step], where stop is excluded and all three parts are optional.

Negative indices count from the end, so s[-1] is the last element and s[-3:] the last three. A negative step walks backwards, which makes s[::-1] the idiomatic reverse — a very common interview one-liner.

Two things worth adding: slicing never raises for out-of-range bounds, it just gives you what exists, which is why a slice bug is silent where an index bug is loud. And a slice of a list is a shallow copy, which is the usual way people copy a list — unlike a NumPy slice, which is a view.
s[::-1]        # reverse
s[-3:]         # last three
s[::2]         # every other one
new = old[:]   # shallow copy of a list
Q34
What is falsy in Python?
Answer
False, None, zero of any numeric type, and every empty container — "", [], (), {}, set(). Everything else is truthy.

Which gives the idiom if not items: rather than if len(items) == 0:.

Q35
What is the difference between remove, pop and del?

Arrays against lists

Q01
Why use a NumPy array instead of a Python list?
Answer
Three reasons, in order of how much interviewers care:

1. Vectorised operations. arr * 2 works on the whole array with no loop; a list would repeat itself instead.
2. Speed. One fixed type means the work runs as compiled C rather than interpreted Python.
3. Memory. A list holds pointers to objects; an array holds the values in one contiguous block.

The cost is that an array holds a single type.
import numpy as np
print(np.array([10,20,30]) * 2)   # [20 40 60]
print([10,20,30] * 2)             # the list REPEATS
Q02
What is broadcasting?
Answer
NumPy stretching a smaller array to match a larger one so an operation can run element by element, without copying the data.

The rule: compared from the right, each dimension must either match or be 1. A single number broadcasts to anything; a row of 3 broadcasts down a 2×3; a length-2 array against a 3-wide table raises ValueError.
m = np.array([[10,20,30],[40,50,60]])
print(m + 100)                # scalar to every element
print(m + np.array([1,2,3]))  # row reused for both rows
Q03
What is the difference between a copy and a view?
Answer
A slice of an array is a view — it shares memory, so changing the slice changes the original. .copy() gives an independent array.

This surprises people coming from lists, where a slice is a copy. It is a common source of bugs that look impossible.
a = np.array([1,2,3,4])
v = a[0:2]; v[0] = 99
print(a)          # [99 2 3 4]  - the original changed
c = a[0:2].copy() # independent

Everyday operations

Q04
What do ndim, shape, size and dtype tell you?
Answer
ndim how many dimensions. shape rows and columns as a tuple. size the total number of elements. dtype the single type they all share.

They are attributes, not methods — no brackets. Printing shape is the first move when a broadcast fails.
Q05
What does axis mean?
Answer
The direction an operation collapses. axis=0 works down the rows and gives one value per column; axis=1 works across and gives one per row.

The memorable form: axis is the direction that disappears.
m = np.array([[10,20,30],[40,50,60]])
print(m.sum(axis=0))   # [50 70 90]  per column
print(m.sum(axis=1))   # [60 150]    per row
Q06
How do you filter a NumPy array?
Answer
A condition returns a boolean array; putting it inside the brackets selects where it is True. Combine conditions with & and |, each in its own brackets — and does not work on arrays.
a = np.array([5,12,18,7,25])
print(a[a > 10])                    # [12 18 25]
print(a[(a > 10) & (a < 20)])       # [12 18]
print((a > 10).sum())               # 3  - True counts as 1
Q07
What is the difference between np.where and boolean filtering?
Answer
Boolean filtering selects; np.where replaces. That is the whole distinction and it is worth saying in those words.

a[a > 60000] returns a shorter array containing only the matching values, so you have lost the positions and can no longer line it up with anything else. np.where(a > 60000, a, 60000) returns an array of the same length, with every element either kept or substituted — so it drops straight back into a DataFrame column.

Which to use follows from that: filter when you want a subset to analyse, np.where when you are building a column. np.where is the vectorised if-else, and it is the same idea as CASE WHEN in SQL and IF in Excel.

Called with one argument, np.where(cond) returns the indices where the condition is true, which is a third behaviour from the same function and surprises people.
np.where(a > 10, 'high', 'low')   # same length, labels
a[a > 10]                         # shorter, values only
Q08
What is the difference between reshape and flatten?
Answer
reshape rearranges the same values into different dimensions — rows × columns must equal size. flatten collapses any array into one dimension and returns a copy, so the original is unchanged.

Creating arrays

Q09
What is the difference between arange and linspace?
Answer
arange takes a step and stops before the end value, exactly like Python's range. linspace takes a count and includes both endpoints.

Use arange for integer sequences and linspace whenever the number of points matters — plotting a smooth curve, building equal-width bins. The reason the distinction matters: arange with a fractional step accumulates floating-point error and can give you one element more or fewer than you expected. linspace cannot, because you told it the count.
np.arange(0, 1, 0.25)      # [0.   0.25 0.5  0.75]       - stop excluded
np.linspace(0, 1, 5)       # [0.   0.25 0.5  0.75 1.  ]  - both ends, 5 points
Q10
Name the ways to create an array without typing the values.
Answer
np.zeros(shape), np.ones(shape) and np.full(shape, value) for a constant. np.eye(n) for an identity matrix. np.arange and np.linspace for sequences. np.random.rand and friends for random values.

The _like family is the one worth knowing by name: np.zeros_like(a) gives you zeros in the same shape and dtype as a. It is how you allocate a result array that is guaranteed to match your input, without repeating the shape and risking a mismatch.
np.zeros((2, 3))
np.full((2, 2), 7)
np.eye(3)
np.zeros_like(a)      # same shape AND dtype as a
Q11
Why is a NumPy operation faster than the same loop in Python?
Answer
Three reasons, and a good candidate gives more than one.

1. The loop runs in C, not Python. a + b drops into compiled code and comes back once, instead of paying Python's interpreter overhead a million times.
2. The data is one contiguous block of one type. A Python list holds pointers to objects scattered across memory; an array is a solid run of bytes, so the CPU cache works for you instead of against you.
3. No boxing. Every Python integer is a full object with a header; an int64 array element is eight bytes.

The name for writing code this way is vectorisation, and the practical rule is: if you are writing a for loop over a NumPy array, there is almost certainly a function that does it for you.
Q12
What is dtype and why should you care?
Answer
The single type every element in the array shares. It drives three things that bite in practice.

Memory — int8 is one byte per element, int64 is eight. On a large array that is the difference between fitting in memory and not.
Overflow — a fixed-width integer wraps round rather than growing. Add 1 to the largest int8 and you get −128, with a warning at most. Python's own integers never do this, so it catches people.
Missing values — np.nan is a float. An integer array cannot hold it, which is why introducing a missing value silently promotes your column to float64.
np.array([1, 2, 3]).dtype          # int64
np.array([1, 2, 3.0]).dtype        # float64 - one float promotes everything
np.array([1, 2, "3"]).dtype        # <U21   - one string makes it all text

Q13
What is the difference between sort and argsort?
Answer
sort returns the values in order. argsort returns the positions that would put them in order.

argsort is the more useful of the two, because the positions let you reorder other arrays the same way — sort salaries and carry the names along with them. That is exactly what sort_values does for you in pandas, and knowing argsort is underneath it explains why pandas can sort a whole DataFrame by one column.

Note also a.sort() sorts in place and returns None, while np.sort(a) returns a new array. Assigning the result of the in-place one is a common slip.
s = np.array([30, 10, 20])
np.sort(s)        # [10 20 30]
np.argsort(s)     # [1 2 0]  - position 1 is smallest
names[np.argsort(s)]   # reorder a different array by the same order

How do you stack two arrays together?
Answer
np.concatenate([a, b], axis=...) is the general tool and the one to reach for, because you have to name the axis and therefore have to think about it. np.vstack stacks vertically (adds rows) and np.hstack horizontally (adds columns) — convenient, and they also handle 1-D inputs more forgivingly.

The requirement to state: every dimension except the one you are joining along must match. Two arrays with 3 and 4 columns cannot be stacked vertically. That mismatch is almost always a reshape you forgot.
np.vstack([a, b])          # more rows

np.concatenate([a, b], axis=0)   # the explicit version

How do you find the unique values and how often each occurs?

Q16
How do you get the median and percentiles?
Answer
np.median, and np.percentile(a, [25, 50, 75]) for quartiles — which gives you the box plot's box in one call. np.quantile is the same thing taking fractions instead of percentages.

np.percentile(s, [25, 50, 75])
np.nanpercentile(s, [25, 50, 75])   # when the data has gaps
Q17
What does np.random.seed do, and why would an interviewer ask?
Answer
It fixes the starting point of the pseudo-random generator, so the same 'random' numbers come out every time you run the code.

Why it matters is the real answer: reproducibility. Without a seed, your train/test split, your sample, your bootstrap all change on every run, so nobody — including you tomorrow — can reproduce the number you reported. With a seed, the analysis is a fact rather than an event.

The modern form is rng = np.random.default_rng(42), which gives you a generator object instead of setting hidden global state. Mentioning that is a small sign of someone who reads documentation.
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
rng.integers(1, 7, size=5)      # same five numbers every run
Q18
How do you multiply two matrices, and what is the trap?
Answer
* multiplies element by element. @ (or np.dot / np.matmul) does matrix multiplication.

The trap is that for two same-shaped square arrays both operators work and give different answers, so the bug is silent. For matrix multiplication the inner dimensions must agree — (2, 3) @ (3, 4) gives (2, 4) — and getting a shape error is the lucky outcome.
a * b      # element-wise
a @ b      # matrix product
a.T        # transpose, often what the shape error wanted
Q19
How do you find where the maximum value is, not just what it is?
Answer
argmax returns the position of the maximum; max returns the value. The pattern names[values.argmax()] answers 'who earns the most' in one line, and it is the NumPy equivalent of idxmax in pandas.

Two things to add. On a 2-D array, bare argmax flattens first and gives you an index into the flattened array — pass axis= or use np.unravel_index. And ties go to the first occurrence, silently.
s.argmax()                  # position of the largest
names[s.argmax()]           # who it belongs to
s.argmax(axis=1)            # per row, on a 2-D array
Q20
What is the difference between flatten and ravel?
Answer
Both turn any array into one dimension. flatten always returns a copy; ravel returns a view when it can.

So ravel is cheaper, and writing to the result of ravel may change your original array, while writing to a flatten result never does. reshape(-1) behaves like ravel.

This is the same copy-versus-view distinction as slicing, and it is the most reliable way to tell whether someone has actually debugged NumPy code or only read about it.
a.ravel()      # view if possible - cheap, but shares memory
a.flatten()    # always a copy - safe, costs memory
a.reshape(-1)  # like ravel

Expressiveness. Arrays support vectorised operations, broadcasting and boolean masking, so whole operations are written without a for loop at all. And Pandas is built on top of NumPy, so you are using it either way.

Q22
Explain broadcasting from first principles, and give me the rule.long answer
Answer
Broadcasting is how NumPy does arithmetic between arrays of different shapes without you writing a loop and without copying data.

The rule: compare shapes from the right-hand end. Two dimensions are compatible if they are equal, or if one of them is 1. A dimension of size 1 is stretched to match. If a dimension is missing on the left, it is treated as 1. Anything else is an error.

Nothing is actually copied — NumPy just reuses the same values as it walks the memory, which is why broadcasting is both fast and memory-cheap.

The everyday use is a scalar: arr * 2 is broadcasting, with the 2 stretched to the whole array. The useful case is subtracting a per-column mean from every row in one line.

  6. arr + 2 is the simplest case of it

Tests whether a candidate can interpret a number, not calculate one. Expect descriptive measures, spread, correlation, sampling and hypothesis testing — explained in plain words.

The basics

Q01
What is the difference between descriptive and inferential statistics?
Answer
Descriptive summarises the data you actually have — you measured everybody, so you cannot be wrong, only unclear. Inferential uses a sample to make a claim about a population you did not measure, so there is always uncertainty attached.

The giveaway is the wording: 'our 200 employees average 38' is descriptive; 'employees in this industry average 38' is inferential and needs a confidence interval.
Q02
What are the four scales of measurement?
Answer
Nominal — names with no order (city, department).
Ordinal — ordered, but the gaps are not equal (Poor / Average / Good).
Interval — equal gaps, no true zero (temperature in Celsius).
Ratio — equal gaps and a true zero, so ratios make sense (salary, age, height).

The test for ratio is whether zero means 'none of it'. 20°C is not twice as hot as 10°C; ₹20,000 is twice ₹10,000.
Q03
Mean, median or mode — which would you report?
Answer
Mean when the data is roughly symmetric. Median when it is skewed or has outliers, because it is not dragged by extremes. Mode for categories, where a mean is meaningless.

The way to show judgement is to report the gap: if mean and median disagree sharply, that disagreement is itself the finding and you should say what caused it.
Q04
What is the difference between variance and standard deviation?
Answer
Variance is the average squared distance from the mean; standard deviation is its square root.

You report standard deviation because it is in the same units as the data — rupees, not rupees squared. Variance exists because squaring stops positive and negative deviations cancelling, and it behaves well mathematically.
Q05
Why divide by n−1 instead of n?
Answer
Because when you estimate the population variance from a sample, using the sample mean makes the deviations slightly too small — the sample mean sits in the middle of its own data by construction. Dividing by n−1 corrects that bias.

If you genuinely have the whole population, divide by n. Note that NumPy defaults to n and pandas defaults to n−1, which is why the two can give slightly different answers on the same column.
Q06
What is skewness?
Answer
Asymmetry. Right (positive) skew has a long tail of high values and pulls the mean above the median — income is the standard example. Left skew is the reverse.

The quick diagnostic needs no formula: compare mean and median. Mean > median means right skew.

Spread and outliers

Q07
What is an outlier and how do you find one?
Answer
A value far from the rest of the data. Two standard rules: the 1.5×IQR fences (below Q1−1.5×IQR or above Q3+1.5×IQR), or a z-score beyond about ±3.

IQR is the safer default, because the mean and standard deviation a z-score depends on are themselves distorted by the outlier.
Q08
Should you always remove outliers?
Answer
No, and this is the question that separates candidates.

Distinguish an impossible value from an extreme but real one. An age of −4 is a data-entry error — fix or remove it. A genuine very high salary is real data; removing it is editing the truth. Often the right move is to keep it, report the median alongside the mean, and say in the report that it is there.
Q09
What is the five-number summary?
Answer
Minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum — exactly what a box plot draws. The box spans Q1 to Q3 (the middle 50%, the IQR), the line inside is the median, the whiskers reach the furthest non-outlier points, and separate dots are outliers.
Q10
What is the empirical rule?
Answer
For a roughly normal distribution, about 68% of values lie within one standard deviation of the mean, 95% within two and 99.7% within three.

It is a fast sanity check: if your data claims 95% within one standard deviation, the distribution is not normal or something is wrong.
Q11
What is a z-score?
Answer
How many standard deviations a value sits from the mean: z = (x − mean) / sd.

Its real use is making incomparable things comparable — a mark of 78 in one exam and 65 in another can be ranked once both are expressed as z-scores.

Relationships

Q12
What is correlation, and what does the number mean?
Answer
A measure of how two numeric variables move together, from −1 to +1. +1 perfect positive, 0 none, −1 perfect negative. Roughly: below 0.3 weak, 0.3–0.7 moderate, above 0.7 strong.

Correlation only measures a linear relationship. A perfect U-shape can correlate at nearly zero.
Q13
What is the difference between Pearson and Spearman?
Answer
Pearson uses the actual values and measures a linear relationship. Spearman converts both columns to ranks first, so it measures whether they move together in order, not in proportion.

Spearman is the right choice with outliers, with skewed data, or with ordinal data. A big gap between the two is diagnostic: it usually means one extreme point is flattening Pearson.
Q14
Correlation is not causation — explain with an example.
Answer
Two variables moving together does not mean one causes the other. Three alternatives to rule out:

Coincidence — with enough variables, some will correlate by chance.
Reverse causation — B may be causing A.
A confounder — ice cream sales and drowning both rise in summer; the weather drives both.

Only a controlled experiment, or a design that handles confounders, supports a causal claim.
Q15
What is covariance, and why is correlation preferred?
Answer
Covariance measures whether two variables move together, but its magnitude depends on the units — a covariance in rupee-years is uninterpretable and changes if you switch to thousands.

Correlation is covariance divided by both standard deviations, which strips the units out and bounds it to −1..+1. That is why you can compare correlations and cannot compare covariances.

Sampling and inference

Q16
What is the difference between a population and a sample?
Answer
The population is everyone you want to describe; the sample is the subset you actually measured. A measure of a population is a parameter, a measure of a sample is a statistic.

Inferential statistics is entirely about how far you can trust a statistic as an estimate of a parameter.
Q17
Name the sampling methods.
Answer
Simple random — everyone equally likely.
Systematic — every kth record.
Stratified — split into groups and sample within each, preserving proportions.
Cluster — sample whole groups rather than individuals, usually for cost.
Convenience — whoever is easiest, and the one most likely to be biased.

Stratified is the one to mention when a subgroup is small and you need it represented.
Q18
What is sampling bias? Give an example.
Answer
When the way you selected the sample makes it unrepresentative, so no sample size fixes it.

The standard example is the 1936 Literary Digest poll: a huge sample drawn from car and telephone owners — who were wealthier than average — predicted the wrong winner, while a far smaller but representative poll got it right. Size does not cure bias. Survivorship bias and non-response bias are the other two worth naming.
Q19
What is the Central Limit Theorem and why does it matter?
Answer
Take repeated samples and the distribution of their means approaches a normal distribution as the sample size grows — even when the underlying data is not normal. Usually n ≥ 30 is treated as enough.

It matters because it is the licence for everything that follows: confidence intervals and hypothesis tests assume the sampling distribution is normal, and the CLT is why that assumption is reasonable on skewed real data.
Q20
What is a confidence interval?
Answer
A range that expresses the uncertainty in an estimate. A 95% interval means that if you repeated the sampling many times, about 95% of the intervals built this way would contain the true value.

It does not mean there is a 95% probability the true value is in this particular interval — stating that correctly is a good signal. A wider interval means less precision; it narrows as the sample grows.

Hypothesis testing

Q21
Explain hypothesis testing in plain words.
Answer
You assume nothing is going on — the null hypothesis — and ask how likely your data would be if that were true. If it would be very unlikely, you reject the null in favour of the alternative.

The steps: state the hypotheses, pick a significance level (usually 0.05), compute the test statistic and p-value, compare, conclude. You never prove the alternative; you only find the null implausible.
Q22
What is a p-value? Define it carefully.
Answer
The probability of seeing a result at least as extreme as the one you got, assuming the null hypothesis is true.

It is not the probability the null is true, and not the probability your result happened by chance. Getting that distinction right is one of the highest-signal answers in a stats round.

Small p-value → the data would be surprising under the null → reject it.
Q23
What are Type I and Type II errors?
Answer
Type I (false positive) — rejecting a null that was actually true. Its probability is α, the significance level you chose.
Type II (false negative) — failing to reject a null that was false. Its probability is β, and 1−β is the power.

They trade off: lowering α to 0.01 reduces false positives and increases false negatives. A medical screening example makes it concrete — which error costs more depends entirely on the context.
Q24
What does statistical significance actually tell you?
Answer
Only that the effect is unlikely to be noise. It says nothing about whether the effect is large enough to matter.

With a big enough sample, a trivial difference becomes significant. So always report the effect size next to the p-value, and ask whether the difference would change a decision.
Q25
Which test would you use, and when?
Answer
One-sample t-test — a sample mean against a known value.
Two-sample t-test — comparing the means of two independent groups.
Paired t-test — before and after on the same subjects.
Chi-square — a relationship between two categorical variables.
ANOVA — comparing the means of three or more groups.

The deciding questions: how many groups, and is the variable numeric or categorical?
Q26
How would you design an A/B test?
Answer
State the hypothesis and the single metric you will judge it on before starting. Randomly assign users to control and variant so the groups differ only in the change. Work out the sample size and run time in advance, then run it to completion.

The two failure modes to name: peeking — stopping as soon as it looks significant, which inflates false positives — and testing many metrics until one comes out significant, which is p-hacking. Fixing the metric and the duration up front prevents both.

Estimation and error

Q27
What is the difference between a parameter and a statistic?
Answer
A parameter describes a population and is usually unknowable — the true average salary of every analyst in India. A statistic describes a sample and is what you actually compute.

The whole of inferential statistics is using the second to make a claim about the first, with an honest statement of how wrong it might be. Greek letters for parameters (μ, σ, ρ), Latin for statistics (x̄, s, r).

Why it matters in a job: when you report 'average order value is ₹43,025', that is a statistic. Whether it is a good estimate of next quarter's parameter depends on how the sample was drawn, which is the question nobody asks until the forecast misses.
Q28
What is standard error, and how is it different from standard deviation?
Answer
Standard deviation measures spread in the data. Standard error measures uncertainty in your estimate. Different things that get confused constantly.

SD answers 'how spread out are individual salaries'. SE answers 'how far might my sample average be from the true average'. SE = SD / √n.

Two consequences worth stating. SE shrinks as the sample grows while SD does not — more data makes your estimate sharper, it does not make people more alike. And because of the square root, quadrupling the sample only halves the error, which is the arithmetic behind every 'can we just collect a bit more data' conversation.
SE = SD / sqrt(n)
100 -> 400 samples halves the error, not quarters it
Q29
t-test or z-test — which and why?
Answer
Both ask whether two means differ by more than chance would explain. The difference is what you know about the spread.

z-test — population standard deviation known, or a large sample. t-test — standard deviation estimated from the sample, which is the real-world case. The t distribution has fatter tails to account for that extra uncertainty, and converges on the normal as n grows — past about 30 they agree closely.

So the practical answer: use a t-test. Then name the variety, because the follow-up is coming: one-sample (against a fixed value), two-sample independent (two separate groups), or paired (the same subjects measured twice, like before and after a change). Using an independent test on paired data throws away the pairing and loses power.
Q30
What is ANOVA, and why not just run several t-tests?
Answer
ANOVA tests whether three or more group means differ, by comparing the variance between groups against the variance within them. A large F ratio means the groups are further apart than their internal noise explains.

The reason not to run t-tests pairwise is the real answer: every test carries a false positive risk, and they compound. Four groups means six comparisons; at 5% each, the chance of at least one spurious result is around 26%, not 5%.

ANOVA gives you one test at one error rate. It tells you that something differs but not which pair, so you follow it with a post-hoc test such as Tukey, which corrects for the multiple comparisons.
Q31
What is a chi-square test for?
Answer
Categorical data — counts, not means. Two uses:

Goodness of fit — does one categorical variable match an expected distribution? Are weekend joiners 28.6% of hires as a uniform spread would predict?
Test of independence — are two categorical variables related? Does the customer segment depend on the city?

It works by comparing observed counts against the counts you would expect if there were no relationship. The assumption to mention: it needs reasonably large expected counts in each cell — roughly 5 or more — so a sparse contingency table needs categories combined first, or Fisher's exact test instead.

This is the test for the kind of question the Excel pivot table raises, which is why it is worth having ready.
Q32
One-tailed or two-tailed?
Answer
Two-tailed asks 'is it different'. One-tailed asks 'is it better' and puts the whole 5% in one tail, which makes it easier to reach significance.

Which is why the rule matters: you must choose before you look at the data. Running two-tailed, not quite reaching significance, and then switching to one-tailed is p-hacking, and a sharp interviewer will be listening for exactly that.

Default to two-tailed. A one-tailed test is only honest when a change in the other direction would genuinely lead to the same decision as no change at all — which is rarer than people claim, because a feature that makes things worse is very much something you want to detect.
Q33
What is statistical power, and what drives the sample size you need?
Answer
Power is the probability of detecting an effect that is really there — 1 − the Type II error rate. The convention is 80%.

Four things trade off, and fixing any three determines the fourth: the effect size you want to catch, the significance level, the power, and n. The one people forget is effect size: detecting a 10% lift is easy, detecting a 0.5% lift takes an enormous sample.

The practical consequence to state, because it is what the question is really about: work out the sample size before the test starts. An underpowered test that returns 'no significant difference' has told you nothing — you cannot distinguish 'no effect' from 'not enough data'. And it is why stopping a test early because it looks good is invalid.

Traps in applied analysis

Q34
You tested 20 metrics and one came back significant at p < 0.05. What do you conclude?
Answer
Probably nothing. At a 5% threshold you expect about one false positive in every 20 tests of nothing at all. Finding exactly one significant result among 20 is the expected outcome when no effect exists.

This is the multiple comparisons problem, and it is the most common way a well-run experiment produces a wrong answer. The fixes: declare one primary metric before the test, and correct the threshold for the rest — Bonferroni divides α by the number of tests, which is crude but defensible; Benjamini-Hochberg is less conservative.

The answer the interviewer is hoping for is the sentence 'which metric did we commit to before we started?' A result found while hunting is a hypothesis, not a finding, and the honest next step is to test it properly.
Q35
What is Simpson's paradox?
Answer
A trend that holds in every subgroup and reverses when the groups are pooled — or the other way round.

The mechanism is always the same: a confounder distributed unevenly between the groups. A treatment can have a better recovery rate in both mild and severe cases, and a worse rate overall, because it was given disproportionately to the severe cases.

Why it is the most useful paradox for an analyst to know: it means an aggregate can be honestly computed and still be misleading, and the only defence is to disaggregate. The class version of this was Lakshmi Kumar sitting in Chennai while belonging to Sales in Mumbai — one row distorting a department average and a city average in opposite directions. Whenever a total surprises you, split it.
Q36
What is a confounding variable, and what do you do about it?
Answer
A third variable that influences both the thing you are measuring and the thing you are measuring it against, creating an association that is not causal. Ice cream sales and drownings both rise with temperature.

What you can do, in order of strength: randomise, which handles even the confounders you have not thought of and is why A/B tests are the gold standard; control for it by comparing within strata; adjust for it in a regression.

The honest limit, worth saying: with observational data you can only adjust for confounders you know about and have measured. That is the real reason 'correlation is not causation' is more than a slogan — there is always a variable you did not collect.
Q37
What does a regression coefficient actually mean?
Answer
For each one-unit increase in that predictor, the outcome changes by the coefficient — holding the other predictors constant. That last clause is the whole answer and it is the part people drop.

Three things to add. The intercept is the predicted value when every predictor is zero, which is often meaningless on its own (zero years of experience, zero area). Coefficients carry units, so their size tells you nothing about importance until you standardise them — a coefficient on salary in rupees will look tiny next to one on years. And 'holding others constant' is a statement about the model, not the world; if two predictors always move together, you cannot really hold one fixed.

A coefficient is also not causal. It is a description of the fitted surface.
Q38
What is R-squared, and what does it not tell you?
Answer
The share of variance in the outcome explained by the model. 0.7 means the model accounts for 70% of the variation.

What it does not tell you, which is the real question:
• whether the model is correct — a straight line through a curve can score well while being wrong everywhere;
• whether the relationship is causal;
• whether it will generalise — R² only ever goes up as you add predictors, even random ones, which is what adjusted R² corrects for;
• whether the predictions are useful, which is a question about error in real units, not variance explained.

And a low R² is not automatically a bad model. Predicting human behaviour at 0.3 can be genuinely valuable; predicting a physical process at 0.9 might be a failure. Always look at the residual plot, which shows you the shape R² hides.
Q39
What is multicollinearity, and why does it matter?
Answer
Two or more predictors that are strongly correlated with each other, so the model cannot tell their effects apart.

The symptoms are distinctive: coefficients with wild standard errors, signs that flip when you add or remove a variable, and a model that predicts well overall while no individual coefficient is significant. Diagnose it with a correlation matrix or the variance inflation factor, where a VIF above about 5 to 10 is a flag.

The key nuance, and a good follow-up: it does not hurt prediction, only interpretation. If you just want forecasts, you can often ignore it. If you need to tell a stakeholder which lever to pull, you cannot. Fix it by dropping one of the pair, combining them, or collecting data where they vary independently.
Q40
What is the difference between normalisation and standardisation?
Answer
Normalisation (min-max) rescales to a fixed range, usually 0 to 1. Standardisation (z-score) centres on 0 with a standard deviation of 1 and leaves the range open.

Use standardisation when the method assumes roughly normal data or compares distances — and it is far more robust to outliers, because one extreme value does not compress everything else. Min-max is the one a single outlier ruins: 99 values squashed into the bottom 1% of the range.

The point that matters in practice: fit the scaler on the training data only and apply it to the test set. Scaling before you split leaks information about the test set into training, which is a real and common mistake.
z    = (x - mean) / std          # standardisation
minmax = (x - min) / (max - min)  # normalisation
Q41
What is kurtosis, and how is it different from skewness?
Answer
Skewness is about asymmetry; kurtosis is about the tails.

High kurtosis (leptokurtic) means fatter tails and a sharper peak — extreme values are more common than a normal distribution would predict. Low kurtosis (platykurtic) means thinner tails. Normal is 3, or 0 if the software reports excess kurtosis, so check which convention you are reading.

Why an analyst should care: fat tails mean your rare events are not as rare as the model assumes, so risk estimates built on a normal assumption are too optimistic. Financial returns are the standard example. It is also a reason to prefer the median and the IQR when describing such data.
Q42
How do you separate trend from seasonality in a time series?
Answer
Trend is the long-run direction. Seasonality is a repeating pattern at a fixed period — day of week, month of year. What is left over is the residual.

Practically: a moving average the length of one full season smooths the seasonality out and leaves the trend, which is why a 7-day average is the first thing to put on a daily chart. Decomposition tools split all three at once, additive if the seasonal swing is a constant amount and multiplicative if it is a constant percentage.

The business use is the point: year-on-year comparison handles seasonality for free, which is why 'down 20% on last month' is usually a worse question than 'down 20% on the same month last year'. Confusing a seasonal dip for a trend is one of the most common analyst errors, and the month-on-month revenue table is where it happens.
Q43
What is a residual, and why plot them?
Answer
A residual is actual minus predicted — what the model got wrong on each row.

You plot them because a well-fitting model should leave nothing but noise: residuals scattered randomly around zero, with constant spread. Any pattern is the model telling you what it missed.

What the shapes mean: a curve means the relationship is not linear; a funnel widening to the right means the error grows with the prediction, which breaks the constant variance assumption and makes the confidence intervals wrong; clusters mean a missing categorical variable; a few points far out are your influential observations.

This is why the residual plot beats R² as a diagnostic — one number cannot show you a shape.

Q44
Explain mean, median and mode, and tell me how you choose between them.long answer
Answer
All three answer “what is a typical value”, and they disagree the moment the data is not symmetrical. Mean adds everything and divides. Median sorts the values and takes the middle one. Mode is the most frequent value.

The choice is not a matter of taste. The mean is pulled by extreme values and the median is not. Report the mean and you have described a company almost nobody works at.

  3. The median only cares about position, so it is resistant to outliers
  4. Mode is the only one that works on categorical data — you cannot average 'Chennai'
  5. A rule they can state: symmetric data → mean. Skewed data or outliers → median
  6. Bonus: report both, because the gap between them is itself the finding

Q45
Explain variance and standard deviation. Why do we square the differences?long answer
Answer
Both measure spread — how far the values sit from their own mean. You take each value's distance from the mean, square those distances, and average them. That average is the variance. Take its square root and you get the standard deviation.

We square for two reasons. First, distances above and below the mean would otherwise cancel to exactly zero — that is a property of the mean, not an accident. Second, squaring makes large deviations count much more than small ones, which is usually what we want.

We then take the square root so the answer is back in the original units. Variance of salary is in rupees-squared, which means nothing to anybody. Standard deviation is in rupees.
Must mention — tick these off
  1. Both measure spread around the mean
  2. Raw deviations sum to zero — that is why you cannot just average them
  3. Squaring removes the sign and punishes big deviations more
  4. SD is the square root of variance, and is in the original units
  5. Variance is in squared units, which is why we rarely report it
  6. Both are as outlier-sensitive as the mean, because both are built on it
Probe if they stall. “Why not just use the average of the absolute distances instead?” — a good student will say that exists (mean absolute deviation) and that squaring has nicer mathematical properties and is what the rest of statistics is built on.
Sounds right, is wrong. “Standard deviation tells you the average distance from the mean.” Close, and useful as intuition, but not literally true — that is the mean absolute deviation. SD is the root of the mean squared distance, and it is always a little larger.
Q46
What is skew? How does it show up in the mean and the median?long answer
Answer
Skew is asymmetry. A symmetric distribution has the same shape on both sides of its centre; a skewed one has a tail stretching further on one side.

Right-skewed (positive skew) means the tail runs to the right, towards the big values. The few large values drag the mean up, so mean > median. Salary is the classic case, and our file is a textbook example: 95,830 against 68,841.

Left-skewed (negative skew) is the mirror image — a tail of small values, and mean < median. Exam marks where most people do well and a few fail badly.
Must mention — tick these off
  1. Skew = asymmetry, named after the direction of the tail, not the bulk
  2. Right / positive skew → mean > median
  3. Left / negative skew → mean < median
  4. Symmetric → mean ≈ median ≈ mode
  5. Comparing mean and median is the cheapest skew test there is — no formula needed
  6. Real-world right-skew: income, house prices, waiting times, file sizes
Probe if they stall. “Our mean salary is 95,830 and our median is 68,841. Which way is it skewed, and what does that tell you about the company?”
Sounds right, is wrong. Students reliably name the direction backwards, because the bulk of the data sits on the left in a right-skewed distribution. Make them say it out loud: the name follows the tail.
Q47
Explain the empirical rule, and tell me when you are not allowed to use it.long answer
Answer
For data that is approximately normal, roughly 68% of values fall within one standard deviation of the mean, 95% within two, and 99.7% within three. It is a quick way to say whether a value is unusual without doing any real work.

The condition is doing all the work. The rule is a property of the normal distribution, not of data in general. Apply it to our salary column, which is heavily right-skewed, and mean minus two standard deviations is a negative salary — which tells you immediately that the assumption has failed.
Must mention — tick these off
  1. 68 / 95 / 99.7 for one, two and three standard deviations
  2. It only holds for data that is approximately normal
  3. Check the shape first — histogram, or compare mean and median
  4. On skewed data it gives impossible bounds, like a negative salary
  5. For non-normal data use percentiles or the IQR instead, which assume nothing
  6. It is the reason '2 standard deviations' is a common outlier rule
Probe if they stall. “Our salary mean is 95,830 with a huge standard deviation. What does mean minus two SD give you, and what does that result tell you about the rule?”
Sounds right, is wrong. “95% of data is always within two standard deviations.” Only for normal data. Chebyshev's inequality gives a much weaker guarantee (at least 75%) for any distribution — mentioning that is a strong answer.
Q48
Explain covariance and correlation, and the difference between Pearson and Spearman.long answer
Answer
Covariance tells you the direction two variables move in together — positive means they rise together, negative means one rises as the other falls. Its size is meaningless on its own because it depends on the units: covariance of salary and experience is in rupee-years.

Correlation is covariance divided by both standard deviations, which strips the units out and forces the answer between -1 and +1. That is why we report correlation and almost never covariance.

Pearson measures linear association and uses the actual values, so it is sensitive to outliers. Spearman throws away the values and uses their ranks, so it measures whether the relationship is consistently increasing — monotonic, not necessarily straight — and barely notices outliers.
Must mention — tick these off
  1. Covariance gives direction only; its magnitude is unit-dependent and unreadable
  2. Correlation is standardised covariance, bounded -1 to +1
  3. 0 means no linear relationship — not 'no relationship'
  4. Pearson: values, linear, outlier-sensitive. Spearman: ranks, monotonic, robust.
  5. Use Spearman for ordinal data, or when there are outliers, or a curved relationship
  6. Neither one implies causation
Probe if they stall. “If Pearson comes out near zero but the scatter plot shows a clear U-shape, what has happened?” — a strong answer: the relationship is real but not linear, so Pearson cannot see it. Always plot it.
Sounds right, is wrong. “Correlation of 0 means the variables are independent.” No. It means no linear relationship. A perfect parabola has a Pearson correlation of about zero.
Q49
Explain the Central Limit Theorem in your own words, and why anybody should care.long answer
Answer
Take repeated random samples of the same size from a population, work out the mean of each sample, and look at how those sample means are distributed. The Central Limit Theorem says that distribution becomes approximately normal as the sample size grows — whatever shape the original population had.

That last part is the whole point. Our salary data is violently right-skewed, but the distribution of average salaries from samples of 50 would still be close to normal, centred on the true population mean, with a spread of σ/√n.

Why anybody cares: it is the reason we can put a confidence interval around a sample mean, or run a t-test, without knowing the shape of the population. Nearly all of inferential statistics stands on it.
Must mention — tick these off
  1. It is about the distribution of sample means, not of the raw data
  2. The sample means go normal regardless of the population's shape
  3. Needs a reasonable sample size — n ≥ 30 is the usual rule of thumb
  4. The spread of the sample means is σ/√n, the standard error
  5. Bigger samples → narrower spread, but by the square root, so 4× the data for 2× the precision
  6. It is why inference works at all without knowing the population distribution
Probe if they stall. “Does the CLT mean our salary data will become normal if we collect more rows?” — No. More rows just gives you a better picture of the same skewed shape. This question separates the students who understood it from the ones who memorised it.
Sounds right, is wrong. “The CLT says data becomes normal with a large enough sample.” This is the single most common wrong answer in the whole module. The data never becomes normal. The sample means do.
Q50
What is a confidence interval, and what does 95% confidence actually mean?long answer
Answer
A confidence interval is a range built around a sample estimate that is likely to contain the true population value. You report it instead of a single number because a single number from a sample is never exactly right.

The 95% is the part everybody gets wrong. It does not mean there is a 95% probability the true value sits inside this particular interval — the true value is a fixed number, it is either in there or it is not. It means the procedure works 95% of the time: if you repeated the whole sampling exercise many times, about 95 intervals in every 100 would contain the true value.

The interval gets narrower with a bigger sample or less variable data, and wider if you demand more confidence.
Must mention — tick these off
  1. A range for the population value, built from a sample
  2. Built as estimate ± margin of error
  3. 95% describes the long-run reliability of the method, not this one interval
  4. Bigger n → narrower interval (via the standard error, σ/√n)
  5. Higher confidence → wider interval. 99% is wider than 95%
  6. A wide interval is itself a finding — it says you do not have enough data
Probe if they stall. “Would a 99% interval be wider or narrower than a 95% one, and why?” — wider, because to be more certain of catching the value you have to cast a bigger net.
Sounds right, is wrong. “There is a 95% chance the true mean is in this interval.” Extremely common, and strictly wrong. Accept it from a fresher if they can then explain the repeated-sampling version when you push.
Q51
Walk me through the sampling methods you know and when you would use each.long answer
Answer
Sampling exists because measuring everybody is usually impossible or too expensive. The goal is a sample that represents the population, so that what is true of the sample is approximately true of everyone.

Simple random — everybody has an equal chance. Clean and unbiased, but can miss a small group by luck. Systematic — take every k-th record. Easy, but dangerous if the list has a repeating pattern. Stratified — split the population into groups and sample within each. Best when you need every group represented. Cluster — split into natural groups and take whole groups. Cheapest for geography, and the least precise.

Against all of these sits convenience sampling — asking whoever is nearby — which is what most bad surveys actually do.
Must mention — tick these off
  1. The purpose is a representative sample, so findings generalise
  2. Simple random: equal chance for everyone, the baseline
  3. Systematic: every k-th, fails against periodic data
  4. Stratified: sample within groups — guarantees small groups appear
  5. Cluster: take whole groups — cheap, less precise
  6. Convenience sampling is not a method, it is a bias, and it is the common real-world failure
Probe if they stall. “We have 42 people in Marketing and 64 in Sales. If you took a simple random sample of 20, what could go wrong, and which method fixes it?” — Marketing might barely appear; stratified fixes it.

Q52
Explain the Z-score. How would you use it to find outliers, and when would you use the IQR instead?long answer
Answer
A Z-score re-expresses a value as how many standard deviations it is from the mean. Subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation. A Z of +2 means two SD above average; a Z of 0 means exactly average.

Its real use is comparing things measured on different scales — a salary and an age cannot be compared directly, but their Z-scores can. For outliers, the common rule is |Z| > 3.

The catch is that the Z-score is built from the mean and the standard deviation, and both are dragged by the very outlier you are hunting. One 52-lakh salary inflates the SD so much that it can hide itself. On skewed data use the IQR rule instead: anything below Q1 - 1.5×IQR or above Q3 + 1.5×IQR, which is built on quartiles and does not care about extremes.
Must mention — tick these off
  1. Z = (value - mean) / standard deviation
  2. It is a unit-free number, which is why it lets you compare different scales
  3. Common outlier threshold |Z| > 3
  4. The weakness: mean and SD are themselves distorted by outliers — masking
  5. IQR rule: outside Q1 - 1.5×IQR or Q3 + 1.5×IQR
  6. Z assumes roughly normal; IQR assumes nothing — prefer IQR on skewed data
Probe if they stall. “Our salary SD is enormous because of one person. What does that do to everyone else's Z-scores?” — it shrinks them all, so genuine high earners stop looking unusual.
Sounds right, is wrong. “Any value with Z above 3 should be deleted.” Finding an outlier is not permission to delete it. The 52-lakh salary is real data. You investigate, then decide, then say what you did.
Q53
Explain conditional probability, and work an example for me.long answer
Answer
Conditional probability is the probability of A given that you already know B happened. Written P(A|B). Knowing B shrinks the world you are working in — you stop dividing by everybody and start dividing by the people who satisfy B.

Formally P(A|B) = P(A and B) / P(B).

Worked on our file: 200 employees, 49 in Tech, and of those 48 earn above 50,000. P(earns above 50,000 given Tech) = 48/49, about 98%. Across the whole company the figure is 172/198, about 87%. Knowing somebody is in Tech changed the answer, which is exactly what 'conditional' means.
Must mention — tick these off
  1. P(A|B) is the probability of A given B has happened
  2. P(A|B) = P(A and B) / P(B)
  3. The denominator changes from the whole population to just the B group
  4. If P(A|B) = P(A), then A and B are independent — knowing B told you nothing
  5. P(A|B) is not P(B|A) — confusing them is the classic medical-test error
  6. A worked example with real numbers beats a formula
Probe if they stall. “What is the probability somebody is in Tech given that they earn above 50,000? Is that the same number as before?” — 48/172, about 28%. Completely different, which proves the two conditionals are not interchangeable.

Q54
What is the difference between descriptive and inferential statistics?long answer
Answer
Descriptive statistics summarise the data you actually have. Counts, averages, medians, standard deviations, charts. There is no uncertainty in a descriptive number: if the mean salary of these 200 people is 95,830, that is simply a fact about these 200 people.

Inferential statistics use a sample to make a claim about a larger population you did not measure. Confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, p-values. Every inferential statement carries uncertainty, because you are reasoning beyond your data.

The dividing line is the question “am I describing these rows, or using these rows to talk about rows I have never seen?”

  2. Inferential = generalising to a population; always uncertain
  3. Inference needs a sample, and the sample must be representative

  5. Inferential tools: confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, p-values

Q55
“Correlation does not imply causation.” Explain properly what that means and what to do about it.long answer
Answer
A correlation says two things move together. It says nothing about one causing the other, because there are at least four explanations for the same number.

One: A really does cause B. Two: B causes A — the arrow points the other way. Three: some third variable causes both — a confounder. Four: pure coincidence, which is common when you test many pairs at once.

The classic example: ice cream sales correlate with drownings. Neither causes the other — hot weather causes both. In our data, experience and salary correlate, but education, department and seniority all sit behind that relationship.

What you actually do about it: run a controlled experiment if you can, and if you cannot, control for the confounders you can name and state plainly that the result is associational.
Must mention — tick these off
  1. Correlation is co-movement, nothing more
  2. Four explanations: A→B, B→A, a confounder causes both, or coincidence
  3. Confounder is the word to use — the hidden third variable
  4. A concrete example, ideally from their own data
  5. A randomised experiment is what establishes causation
  6. Failing that: control for what you can and say it is an association
Probe if they stall. “Departments with higher average experience also have higher average salary. Name me a third variable that could be driving both.”

The rules

Q01
What is the difference between theoretical and empirical probability?
Answer
Theoretical comes from reasoning about the setup — a fair coin is 0.5 because there are two equally likely outcomes. Empirical comes from observation — you flipped it 1,000 times and got 0.512.

As the number of trials grows, empirical approaches theoretical. That is the law of large numbers.
Q02
What are mutually exclusive and independent events?
Answer
Mutually exclusive — they cannot both happen. A single card cannot be both a king and a queen, so P(A and B) = 0.
Independent — one happening does not change the probability of the other. Two coin flips.

They are different ideas and are often confused. In fact two mutually exclusive events with non-zero probability are necessarily dependent — knowing one happened tells you the other did not.
Q03
State the addition and multiplication rules.
Answer
Addition (or): P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). You subtract the overlap so it is not counted twice. If they are mutually exclusive the overlap is zero and it simplifies.
Multiplication (and): P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B|A). If they are independent, P(B|A) = P(B) and it simplifies to the product.
Q04
What is conditional probability?
Answer
The probability of A given that B has happened: P(A|B) = P(A and B) / P(B). You have narrowed the world down to the cases where B is true, so B becomes the new denominator.

The key insight: P(A|B) and P(B|A) are not the same, and confusing them is behind most probability mistakes.

Distributions

Q05
Describe the normal distribution.
Answer
Symmetric, bell-shaped, with mean, median and mode all at the centre. It is defined entirely by its mean and standard deviation, and the area under the curve is 1.

The empirical rule follows from it: roughly 68, 95 and 99.7 percent of values within one, two and three standard deviations. It matters because the Central Limit Theorem makes sample means normal even when the raw data is not.
Q06
What is a standard normal distribution?
Answer
A normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 1, written Z. Any normal variable becomes standard normal by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation — which is exactly what computing a z-score does.

Why it exists: it gives you one table or function that works for every normal distribution. Rather than needing probabilities for salaries in rupees and heights in centimetres separately, you convert to Z and look up once.

The numbers to have memorised, because they are asked directly: ±1 covers about 68%, ±2 about 95%, ±3 about 99.7%. And 1.96 is the exact figure for 95%, which is where the 1.96 in every confidence interval comes from.
Q07
What is the difference between a discrete and a continuous distribution?
Answer
Discrete variables take countable values — number of employees, dice rolls — and you can ask for the probability of an exact value. Continuous variables take any value in a range — height, salary — and the probability of any single exact value is zero; you can only ask about a range.

That is why probability for continuous variables is an area under the curve.
Q08
What is the difference between probability and odds?
Answer
Probability is successes over all outcomes; odds are successes over failures.

A probability of 0.2 is odds of 1 to 4 — 0.2 / 0.8. Probability runs from 0 to 1; odds run from 0 to infinity. Converting back is odds / (1 + odds).

Two reasons an analyst meets odds. Logistic regression reports coefficients as log-odds, and exponentiating one gives an odds ratio — so interpreting that output requires this distinction. And odds are how betting markets and a lot of medical literature quote risk.

The trap worth naming: an odds ratio is not a relative risk, and the two are routinely reported as if interchangeable. They are close when the event is rare and diverge badly when it is common, which is how a headline turns a modest effect into a dramatic one.

Counting and expectation

Q09
What is expected value, and what does it not tell you?
Answer
The long-run average: multiply each outcome by its probability and add them up. A fair die has an expected value of 3.5 — a number you can never actually roll, which is the point. Expectation describes the average of many trials, not any single one.

What it hides is spread. A certain ₹100 and a coin flip between ₹0 and ₹200 have the same expected value and are not remotely the same offer. Any business answer built on an expected value needs the variance next to it, which is exactly why an A/B test reports a confidence interval and not just a mean.
E[X] = sum(outcome x probability)
     = 1(1/6) + 2(1/6) + ... + 6(1/6) = 3.5
Q10
What is the difference between a permutation and a combination?
Answer
Permutation: order matters. Combination: order does not.

Picking a captain and a vice-captain from 11 players is a permutation — swapping the two names gives a different outcome. Picking 2 players for a committee is a combination.

The formulas fall out of that: nPr = n! / (n−r)! and nCr = n! / (r! (n−r)!). The combination divides by the extra r! precisely to remove the orderings you just decided not to count. If a candidate can explain why the r! is there, they understand it.
5P2 = 5!/3!      = 20     # captain and vice-captain
5C2 = 5!/(2!3!)  = 10     # a committee of two
Q11
What is the complement rule, and when is it the easy way in?
Answer
P(not A) = 1 − P(A). Trivial as a formula, and it is the single most useful shortcut in probability.

The tell is the phrase 'at least one'. Computing 'at least one head in ten flips' directly means adding up ten separate cases. Computing it as 1 − P(no heads at all) is one multiplication. Any time the question says 'at least', try the complement first.
P(at least one 6 in 4 rolls)
  = 1 - P(no 6 at all)
  = 1 - (5/6)^4
  = 0.518
Q12
What is a random variable, and what is the difference between discrete and continuous?
Answer
A random variable is just a number attached to an uncertain outcome — the number of heads in ten flips, tomorrow's rainfall, a customer's basket value.

Discrete variables take countable values, so you can ask for the probability of an exact value: P(exactly 3 heads) is a real, non-zero number. Continuous variables take any value in a range, and the probability of any exact value is zero — you can only ask about intervals. That is the whole reason continuous distributions use areas under a curve instead of heights.
Q13
PMF, PDF and CDF — what is each for?
Answer
PMF (probability mass function) — discrete. Height = probability of exactly that value. The bars add to 1.
PDF (probability density function) — continuous. Height is density, not probability; probability is the area under it. The total area is 1, and a density can exceed 1 where the range is narrow.
CDF (cumulative distribution function) — either. Height = P(X ≤ x), so it climbs from 0 to 1 and never falls.

In practice you use the CDF for everything, because 'what fraction is below this value' is the question people actually ask. That is what a percentile is.

The distributions worth knowing

Q14
What is the binomial distribution, and what are its conditions?
Answer
The number of successes in a fixed number of independent yes/no trials with a constant success probability. Four conditions, and they are the answer: fixed n, two outcomes, independent trials, constant p.

Mean np, variance np(1−p). The analyst use is conversion: 500 visitors at a 4% conversion rate gives a mean of 20 purchases and a standard deviation of about 4.4 — which is how you know that 17 purchases tomorrow is normal noise and not a problem to investigate.

The condition that breaks in real life is independence. Visitors arriving from the same campaign, or the same household, are not independent.
Q15
What is the Poisson distribution, and when is it the right model?
Answer
The number of events in a fixed window when events happen independently at a constant average rate: support tickets per hour, defects per batch, website errors per minute.

One parameter, λ, which is both the mean and the variance. That is a useful diagnostic: if your count data has variance much larger than its mean, it is overdispersed and Poisson is the wrong model — usually because the rate is not actually constant.

The link to binomial: Poisson is what binomial becomes when n is large and p is small, which is why rare-event counting lands here.
Q16
What is the uniform distribution?
Answer
Every outcome in the range is equally likely — a flat line. A fair die is discrete uniform; a random number between 0 and 1 is continuous uniform.

Why it is worth knowing: it is the null model. 'If nothing were going on, this would be spread evenly' is the baseline you compare against, and departures from uniform are the finding. The 24% weekend-joining rate in the HR data is exactly that argument — weekends are two sevenths of the week, so uniform would predict 28.6%, and 24% is close enough to say the day of week does not matter much.
Q17
State Bayes' theorem and explain what it is for.
Answer
P(A|B) = P(B|A) × P(A) / P(B).

In words: it flips a conditional probability round. You know how often the test is positive given the disease; you want to know how likely the disease is given a positive test. Those two are wildly different numbers, and confusing them is one of the most consequential errors in applied statistics.

The vocabulary: P(A) is the prior (what you believed before), P(B|A) is the likelihood (what the evidence says), P(A|B) is the posterior (what you believe now). Bayes is how evidence updates belief.
Q18
A test is 99% accurate for a disease that affects 1 in 1,000 people. You test positive. What is the chance you have it?
Answer
About 9% — not 99%. This is the question, and almost everyone's instinct is wrong.

Take 100,000 people. 100 have the disease and the test catches 99 of them. 99,900 do not, and the test wrongly flags 1% of those — 999 people. So 1,098 positives, of which 99 are real: 99 / 1,098 = 9.0%.

The lesson is about base rates, and it is not an academic point. This is why a fraud model with 99% accuracy can still be useless, why screening a rare event produces mostly false positives, and why 'accuracy' is a bad metric for an imbalanced dataset — which is the follow-up the interviewer is heading towards. Counting out of 100,000 rather than quoting the formula is the clearest way to answer.
P(disease | positive)
  = 99 / (99 + 999)
  = 0.090
Q19
What is the law of large numbers, and how is it different from the Central Limit Theorem?
Answer
They get confused constantly, so the distinction is worth having ready.

Law of large numbers — as your sample grows, the sample mean converges on the true mean. It is about one number getting more accurate.
Central Limit Theorem — as your sample grows, the distribution of the sample mean becomes normal, whatever the population looked like. It is about the shape of the uncertainty.

You need the first to believe your estimate, and the second to put a confidence interval round it. And the gambler's fallacy is the law of large numbers misread: a fair coin does not owe you heads, it just dilutes the imbalance as n grows.
Q20
Two dice are rolled. What is the probability the total is 7? And that at least one is a 6?
Answer
The point of this one is whether they count the sample space properly. 36 equally likely outcomes, not 21 — (2, 5) and (5, 2) are different outcomes.

Total of 7: six ways — (1,6) (2,5) (3,4) (4,3) (5,2) (6,1) — so 6/36 = 1/6. It is the most likely total, which is a nice sanity check.

At least one 6: the complement is cleaner. P(no 6) = (5/6)² = 25/36, so the answer is 11/36. A candidate who says 1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3 has double-counted (6, 6) and has just shown you they do not have the addition rule straight.

Formulas and references

Q01
What is the difference between a relative, absolute and mixed reference?
Answer
Relative (A1) shifts when you copy the formula. Absolute ($A$1) never moves. Mixed ($A1 or A$1) locks one part — the dollar locks whatever follows it.

The reason it matters: copying a formula that should point at one fixed cell, without the dollars, makes the reference walk down the sheet. You get wrong numbers and no error.
=K2-Q10      wrong when filled down - Q11, Q12 ...
=K2-$Q$10    correct - Q10 every time
Q02
What is the difference between COUNT, COUNTA and COUNTBLANK?
Answer
COUNT counts numbers only. COUNTA counts anything not empty, including text. COUNTBLANK counts the empty cells.

The gap between COUNTA and COUNT on the same range is your missing numeric data, which is a neat way to answer 'how would you check for blanks?' without conditional formatting.
Q03
Does AVERAGE treat a blank cell as zero?
Answer
No — it skips blanks entirely, so it divides by the count of filled cells. A cell containing 0 is counted.

This matters because =SUM(range)/COUNTA(range) and =AVERAGE(range) can disagree, and the second is almost always the one you want.
Q04
What is the difference between IF and IFS?
Answer
IF handles one test and two outcomes, and can be nested for more. IFS takes a list of condition/result pairs and returns the first that is true — much more readable than five nested IFs.

The trap with IFS is order: it stops at the first match, so a loose condition placed first swallows everything after it.
=IF(K2>50000, "High", "Low")
=IFS(H2<=2,"Junior", H2<=7,"Mid", H2>=0,"Senior")
Q05
Explain COUNTIF, SUMIF, AVERAGEIF and the ...IFS versions.
Answer
COUNTIF(range, condition) counts matches. SUMIF(range_to_test, condition, range_to_add) — note the ranges are different arguments. AVERAGEIF has the same shape.

The ...IFS versions take pairs of range and condition and require all of them to be true. Confusingly, COUNTIFS puts the criteria range first while SUMIFS puts the sum range first — worth checking every time.
=COUNTIF(D2:D201,"Tech")
=SUMIF(D2:D201,"Tech",K2:K201)
=COUNTIFS(D2:D201,"Tech",K2:K201,">50000")
Q06
How do you use a cell value inside a COUNTIF condition?
Answer
Put the operator in quotes and join it to the cell with &. Writing ">$Q$5" inside the quotes makes Excel search for the literal text, which matches nothing and returns 0 with no error.
=COUNTIF(K2:K201, ">"&$Q$5)      correct
=COUNTIF(K2:K201, ">$Q$5")       returns 0, silently

Lookups

Q07
How does VLOOKUP work, and what are its limitations?
Answer
Four arguments: what to find, the table to search, which column number to return, and exact or approximate.

Three limitations to name: it can only look right of the search column; the column index is a hard-coded number that breaks if someone inserts a column; and the fourth argument is optional, so leaving it out silently gives approximate matching.
=VLOOKUP(G2, bonus!$A$2:$B$5, 2, FALSE)
Q08
Why must the last argument of VLOOKUP be FALSE?
Answer
TRUE (or omitting it) means approximate match, which assumes the lookup column is sorted ascending and returns the largest value less than or equal to what you asked for.

On unsorted data that produces confident, wrong answers — some rows right by luck, some #N/A, with no pattern. Approximate matching has a legitimate use for banding (tax brackets, grades), but for an exact lookup it is always FALSE.
Q09
What is the difference between VLOOKUP and XLOOKUP?
Answer
XLOOKUP replaces VLOOKUP, HLOOKUP and INDEX+MATCH. It takes the lookup array and the return array as separate ranges, so it can look in any direction and does not break when a column is inserted. It defaults to exact match, and has a built-in not-found argument instead of needing IFERROR.

The catch: it needs Microsoft 365 or Excel 2021, so VLOOKUP is still what most interviews ask about.
=XLOOKUP(G2, bonus!$A$2:$A$5, bonus!$B$2:$B$5, "Not found")
Q10
What is INDEX + MATCH and why use it?
Answer
MATCH returns the position of a value in a range; INDEX returns the value at a given position. Nested, they do a lookup in any direction and survive column insertion, because MATCH finds the column by name rather than by number.
=INDEX(A2:A201, MATCH("Karthik Reddy", C2:C201, 0))
Q11
What does #N/A mean, and should you wrap everything in IFERROR?
Answer
#N/A means not available — the value you searched for is not in the table. It is usually a data problem, not a formula problem.

No. IFERROR hides the error without fixing the cause, so genuinely missing data disappears behind 'Not found' and nobody investigates. Read the errors first, understand why they are there, and hide them only once you have.
Q12
Name the common Excel errors and what each means.
Answer
#N/A lookup value not found. #REF! the reference points at a deleted cell. #VALUE! wrong data type, usually text where a number was expected. #DIV/0! dividing by zero or an empty cell. #NAME? a misspelt function name. #### is not an error at all — the column is too narrow.

Cleaning and data quality

Q13
You are given a 50,000-row sheet on a shared screen. How do you find duplicates?
Answer
State the two cases, because the interviewer is checking whether you distinguish them.

To see them: select the column, Conditional Formatting → Highlight Cells Rules → Duplicate Values.
To count them: =COUNTIF(A:A, A2) filled down, then filter for > 1.
To remove them: Data → Remove Duplicates — but take a copy of the sheet first, because it deletes permanently.

Mention that Remove Duplicates compares the columns you tick, so ticking all of them means only exact row copies go.
Q14
What is the danger of sorting in Excel?
Answer
Sorting a single column without expanding the selection moves that column's values and leaves every other column in place. The rows come apart and every record is now wrong, with no warning.

Always select the whole table, or use Data → Sort which prompts to expand. And know where Ctrl+Z is.
Q15
How do you remove unwanted spaces, and why does it matter?
Answer
TRIM removes leading and trailing spaces (and collapses internal runs to one). CLEAN removes non-printing characters that come from pasted or exported data.

It matters because a trailing space is invisible but makes two values unequal, which breaks lookups and grouping. =SUBSTITUTE(A2," ","·") is a neat way to see the problem before cleaning it.
Q16
What is Text to Columns, and what is its limitation?
Answer
Data → Text to Columns splits one column into several on a delimiter or a fixed width.

Its limitation is that it is a one-time operation. Add a row later and nothing happens — it is not watching the column. A formula, or Power Query, repeats itself; Text to Columns does not.
Q17
What are the common ways data arrives dirty, and how do you fix each?
Answer
Duplicates → Remove Duplicates. Blanks → decide to drop or fill, and say which. Inconsistent case or spelling ('HR' / 'hr' / 'H.R.') → UPPER/PROPER plus Find & Replace. Numbers stored as text → the green triangle, VALUE, or Text to Columns. Trailing spaces → TRIM. Impossible values (a negative age) → investigate, do not silently delete.

Pivot tables and reporting

Q18
Explain a pivot table and its four areas.
Answer
A tool that summarises a table by dragging fields into four areas:

Rows what goes down the side · Columns what goes across the top · Values the number to calculate · Filters which data to include.

It answers the same question as COUNTIF and SUMIF, and as SQL's GROUP BY, without writing a formula.
Q19
Why does my pivot table show Count when I wanted Sum?
Answer
Excel chooses Count when the column contains any text or blanks, and Sum when it is cleanly numeric.

Fix it with right-click → Summarize Values By → Sum. But read the heading first: 'Count of salary' and 'Sum of salary' are different questions, and the count will be lower than the row count wherever a value is blank.
Q20
Why does a pivot table not update when the data changes?
Answer
Because it is a snapshot, not a live formula. Right-click → Refresh, or Data → Refresh All for every pivot in the workbook.

Worse, adding rows below the source range is not picked up by Refresh at all, because the pivot remembers the original range — you have to change the data source, or base the pivot on a Table so it grows.
Q21
How do you show a percentage of the total in a pivot table?
Answer
Drag the same field into Values a second time, then right-click it → Show Values As → % of Grand Total.

Good practice is to show the count and the percentage. A bare '32%' hides the sample size, and 32% of 10 is a very different claim from 32% of 10,000.
Q22
What is conditional formatting used for?
Answer
Two quite different jobs, and saying both shows range. Finding bad data — highlight negatives, duplicates, blanks. Helping a reader — data bars and colour scales that make a column comparable at a glance.

With data bars, keep the numbers visible: the longest value always fills the cell, which can make a small difference look dramatic.
Q23
What makes a summary sheet good enough to send?
Answer
A title, a date, the number of rows it describes, at least one chart, and a sentence saying what the numbers mean. Put it as the first sheet so the reader does not land on raw data.

The test: could the recipient open it, understand it, and know what matters without asking you a question? That is also a good answer to 'how do you present findings'.

Write the formula

Q24
Write a formula that counts Sales employees earning more than ₹80,000.
Answer
COUNTIFS, and the thing to watch is that each criteria range is paired with its own criterion, in order. Two common slips: writing >80000 without the quotes (a comparison criterion must be a text string), and giving the criteria ranges different heights, which returns #VALUE!.

The follow-up: now make the threshold a cell so the user can change it. That needs string concatenation — ">"&F1 — and it is the single most asked Excel formula question after VLOOKUP, because almost everyone writes ">F1" the first time and gets zero.
=COUNTIFS(C:C, "Sales", E:E, ">80000")

=COUNTIFS(C:C, $F$1, E:E, ">"&$F$2)      -- driven by cells
Q25
Write a two-way lookup: find the value where the row is a department and the column is a month.
Answer
VLOOKUP cannot do this on its own because its column number is fixed. Two good answers:

INDEX with two MATCHes — one finds the row, one finds the column. This is the classic and it works in every version.
XLOOKUP nested — an XLOOKUP that returns a whole row, fed into another that picks the column. Cleaner if they have it.

Lock the ranges with $ before copying, and set the last argument of each MATCH to 0 for an exact match — the default of 1 assumes sorted data and returns a wrong answer rather than an error.
=INDEX($B$2:$M$20, MATCH($P2, $A$2:$A$20, 0), MATCH(Q$1, $B$1:$M$1, 0))

=XLOOKUP(Q$1, $B$1:$M$1, XLOOKUP($P2, $A$2:$A$20, $B$2:$M$20))
Q26
What is SUMPRODUCT and why would you use it?
Answer
It multiplies arrays element by element and sums the result. The obvious use is a weighted total — quantity times price down a column, in one cell, without a helper column.

The less obvious use is why people still teach it: it counts and sums with conditions that COUNTIFS cannot express, because the conditions become arrays of TRUE/FALSE which multiply to 1 and 0. That covers OR logic across different columns, criteria involving a calculation, and counting distinct values.

Today SUMIFS handles most of it more readably and the dynamic array functions handle the rest, so the honest answer is: know it, because you will inherit workbooks full of it, and reach for SUMIFS in anything you write yourself.
=SUMPRODUCT(D2:D100, E2:E100)                        -- weighted total
=SUMPRODUCT((C2:C100="Sales")*(E2:E100>80000))        -- conditional count
Q27
Write a formula to rank each employee's salary within their own department.
Answer
RANK alone ranks across the whole column, so it needs a condition. COUNTIFS does it directly: count how many people in the same department earn more, then add one.

That construction is worth pointing out explicitly, because it is the Excel version of RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY dept) in SQL — and saying so out loud is a strong signal that someone understands both tools rather than having memorised two recipes. Change > to >= and you get the dense behaviour instead.
=COUNTIFS($C$2:$C$200, C2, $E$2:$E$200, ">"&E2) + 1

-- the SQL this mirrors:
-- RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY dept_id ORDER BY salary DESC)
Q28
Write a formula to pull the first name out of a full name, and then the last name.
Answer
First name is straightforward: LEFT up to the position of the space, which FIND gives you, minus one.

Last name is the awkward one, and the trick is to work from the right: the number of characters after the space is LEN minus the position of the space.

Two things to add. FIND is case-sensitive and SEARCH is not, and SEARCH accepts wildcards. And both return #VALUE! when there is no space at all, so a single-word name breaks the column — wrap it in IFERROR only once you have decided what a missing surname should show. TEXTBEFORE and TEXTAFTER do this directly in current Excel.
=LEFT(A2, FIND(" ", A2) - 1)
=RIGHT(A2, LEN(A2) - FIND(" ", A2))

=TEXTBEFORE(A2, " ")    -- newer Excel
=TEXTAFTER(A2, " ")
Q29
Write a formula for tenure in completed years, and one for the end of the current month.
Answer
DATEDIF(start, end, "Y") gives completed years — the Excel equivalent of TIMESTAMPDIFF(YEAR, ...). It is an undocumented function kept for compatibility, it does not autocomplete, and it is still the right answer. (TODAY()-A2)/365 is wrong for the same reason as in SQL.

EOMONTH(TODAY(), 0) is the last day of this month; -1 gives last month, 1 next. It is the clean way to build month-end reporting ranges.

Worth naming too: NETWORKDAYS for working days between two dates with a holiday list, which is how SLA and turnaround calculations should be done.
=DATEDIF(A2, TODAY(), "Y")           -- completed years
=DATEDIF(A2, TODAY(), "YM")          -- leftover months
=EOMONTH(TODAY(), 0)                 -- end of this month
=NETWORKDAYS(A2, B2, Holidays)       -- working days
Q30
What are the dynamic array functions, and what changes because of them?
Answer
UNIQUE, FILTER, SORT, SORTBY, SEQUENCE, TEXTSPLIT, and the # spill reference.

What changes is that one formula returns many cells, which replaces whole workflows. =UNIQUE(C:C) is Remove Duplicates without destroying the source. =FILTER(...) is AutoFilter that updates itself. =SORT(UNIQUE(FILTER(...))) builds a live summary list with no pivot table and no manual refresh.

The caveat that gets you in a real office: these are Microsoft 365 only. A workbook built on them opens as #NAME? on an older Excel, so ask what the recipient has before you build the deliverable on them.
=UNIQUE(C2:C200)
=SORT(FILTER(A2:E200, (C2:C200="Sales")*(E2:E200>80000)), 5, -1)
=COUNTIF(F2#, "Sales")        -- # refers to the whole spilled range

Workbook craft and Power Query

Q31
Why use an Excel Table, and what are structured references?
Answer
Ctrl+T turns a range into a Table, and three things follow.

It grows by itself: a new row is inside the Table, so every formula, chart and pivot built on it includes the row automatically. That alone removes the most common Excel failure, which is a report quietly covering the wrong range.
Formulas become readable — SUM(Sales[Amount]) instead of SUM(E2:E5000), and the name survives insertion and sorting.
Tables are what Power Query and Power Pivot expect as a source.

The one habit to go with it: name the Table something meaningful immediately. A workbook full of Table1 to Table9 is barely better than cell references.
=SUMIFS(Sales[Amount], Sales[Dept], "Sales")
=[@Qty] * [@Price]        -- within a Table row
Q32
What is a named range, and when is it worth the trouble?
Answer
A name that refers to a cell or range, so =TaxRate works anywhere in the workbook instead of =Config!$B$4.

Worth it for assumptions — a rate, a threshold, a target that appears in twenty formulas. The formula then documents itself and the value changes in one place, which is the difference between a model someone can audit and a model nobody dares touch.

Not worth it for data ranges, where a Table is better because it resizes. And the caution: an unused or broken name hides in Name Manager and shows up as #REF! much later, so clean them out.
Q33
VLOOKUP returns #N/A but the value is visibly right there. Why?
Answer
Four causes, and they account for nearly every case. Give them as a checklist, because that is what makes the answer useful.

1. Numbers stored as text. The lookup value is the number 1001 and the table holds the text "1001", or the reverse. They look identical. A left-aligned 'number' or a green triangle is the tell; ISTEXT confirms it.
2. Trailing or leading spaces, usually from a system export. TRIM fixes it, and LEN proves it.
3. The lookup value is not in the first column of the table range, which is the one thing VLOOKUP absolutely requires.
4. The last argument is TRUE or omitted on unsorted data, so it returns an approximate match or fails unpredictably.

Non-printing characters are the fifth and nastiest; CLEAN plus SUBSTITUTE(x, CHAR(160), " ") handles the non-breaking space that arrives with anything copied from a web page.
=TRIM(CLEAN(SUBSTITUTE(A2, CHAR(160), " ")))
=LEN(A2)        -- compare with what you expect
=ISTEXT(A2)     -- is that 'number' really a number?
Q34
A CSV opens with your numbers as text and your dates in the wrong format. What do you do?
Answer
Do not fix it cell by cell. Three routes, best first.

Power Query. Import rather than open, set the type on each column, and set the locale when parsing dates — that is the correct fix for a dd/mm versus mm/dd problem and it is repeatable next month.
Text to Columns. Select the column, run it, and on the final step set the column data format — Date with the right order, or General for numbers. Fast, but manual and destructive.
Formulas. VALUE, DATEVALUE, or multiplying by 1 to coerce. Fine for a one-off column.

The thing to warn about: opening a CSV by double-clicking lets Excel guess, and it mangles leading zeros in pin codes and account numbers, and reads some text as dates. That damage happens before you see the file, and the only reliable prevention is importing rather than opening.
Q35
What is Power Query, and what is the shape of a Power Query solution?
Answer
An ETL tool inside Excel. You connect to a source, apply a sequence of transformation steps, and load the result — and the steps are saved and repeatable, which is the whole point. Next month you press Refresh instead of redoing the cleaning.

The typical step list: connect, promote headers, set data types, remove unneeded columns, filter rows, split or merge columns, group, then merge or append other queries, then Close & Load.

Why it matters more than any formula on this page: it moves you from fixing this file to building a process. It handles files too large for a worksheet, it combines every file in a folder in one query, and the Applied Steps pane is documentation that cannot drift out of date. It is also the same engine as Power BI's, so the skill transfers directly.
Q36
What is the difference between Merge and Append in Power Query?
Answer
Merge adds columns. Append adds rows. They are Power Query's join and union.

Merge matches two queries on a key and offers the same join kinds as SQL — left outer, inner, right, full, and usefully left anti and right anti, which give you the unmatched rows directly. The anti joins are the fastest data-quality check in the tool: a left anti join tells you instantly which keys are missing from the lookup table.

Append stacks queries with the same columns, which is how you combine January to December into one table. The gotcha: Append matches by column name, so one sheet with 'Amt' instead of 'Amount' produces a new column full of nulls rather than an error.
Q37
In Power Query, Remove Blank Rows did not remove the rows with blanks in them. Why?
Answer
Because it does exactly what it says and not what people read it as. Remove Blank Rows removes rows where every column is empty. A row with a name, a department and a missing salary is not a blank row.

To remove rows where one particular column is empty, use the column's filter dropdown and untick (null), or Remove Rows → Remove Errors for failed type conversions — which are a different thing again and often the real problem after setting data types.

This matters beyond the button: it is the same distinction as dropna(how="all") versus dropna(how="any") in pandas, and the default in pandas is any — the opposite. Two tools, opposite defaults, same English word. Worth asking precisely because the obvious answer is wrong.
Q38
What is a calculated field in a pivot table, and when should you not use one?
Answer
A formula evaluated inside the pivot, written in terms of field names rather than cells, so it recalculates as you slice.

The trap is what it operates on: a calculated field applies the formula to the aggregated totals, not row by row. So a margin field computed as (Revenue - Cost) / Revenue is correct, because that works on sums. But an average of a ratio is not the ratio of the averages, and that is where people get a number they cannot explain.

When the calculation must happen per row — anything multiplying two columns together before aggregating — put a helper column in the source data instead and sum it. The general rule: calculate at the grain the arithmetic needs, which is the same lesson as average order value in SQL and pandas.
Q39
What are slicers and timelines, and why use them instead of the filter area?
Answer
A slicer is a set of clickable buttons that filters one or more pivot tables; a timeline is the same thing for dates, with a range slider and month/quarter/year granularity.

Two reasons they beat the report filter dropdown. They show the current selection without being opened, so nobody reads a filtered report thinking it is the whole picture — which is a genuine and frequent reporting accident. And one slicer can be connected to several pivot tables and charts at once, which is how you build a dashboard in plain Excel: one slicer driving four visuals that stay in sync.

The cost is screen space and a little performance on large models.

Explain pivot tables end to end. What are they actually doing?long answer
Answer

Q43
Explain relative, absolute and mixed references, and tell me about a time one goes wrong.long answer
Answer
A reference like A1 is relative — it describes a direction, not a place. Copy it down and it moves with you. $A$1 is absolute and never moves. Mixed locks one half: $A1 fixes the column, A$1 fixes the row. The dollar locks whatever follows it, which is the sentence worth memorising.

The failure is always the same shape. You write a formula that subtracts a single fixed cell — a threshold, a target, a company average — and fill it down without the dollars. The fixed cell walks down with the formula, so row 2 compares against the right cell, row 3 against the one below it, and by row 50 you are comparing against empty cells.

Nothing goes red. You get a column of plausible numbers that are all wrong, and the only way to catch it is to look at one formula in the middle of the column.

  1. Relative moves, absolute does not, mixed locks one half
  2. The dollar locks what comes after it
  3. F4 cycles through the four combinations
  4. The failure: a fixed cell that drifts when filled down
  5. No error appears — the numbers are just wrong
  6. Catch it by clicking a formula in the middle of the filled range
  7. Mixed references are what make a multiplication grid work
Probe if they stall. “Where would you need $A1 rather than $A$1?” — when filling across and down, like a rate table.
Sounds right, is wrong. “I always use absolute references to be safe.” Then the formula cannot be filled at all — every row would reference the same source cell.
Q44
Give me three different ways an average can be wrong, using data you have actually seen.long answer
Answer
One — the outlier. Our company mean salary is 95,830 and the median is 68,841. One employee earning 52 lakh is 27% of the entire salary bill. The mean is arithmetically correct and describes a company almost nobody works at. Tech's average is 1,89,754 against a median of 85,486 for the same reason.

Two — the wrong divisor. AVERAGE skips blanks and divides by 198. Write SUM/200 instead and you have treated two blank salaries as zero, giving 94,871 — a gap of 958 from nothing more than an assumption about what a blank means. A blank is not a zero.

Three — averaging an average. Take the four department averages and average those four numbers, and you have weighted a 42-person department the same as a 64-person one. You must weight by group size, or compute from the raw rows.
Must mention — tick these off
  1. Outliers: mean 95,830 against median 68,841, one person at 27% of the bill
  2. The divisor: AVERAGE skips blanks; SUM/200 treats a blank as zero
  3. Averaging averages without weighting by group size
  4. The mean is arithmetically right and practically useless in case one
  5. Report the median alongside, and say which you trust
  6. A fourth if they are strong: averaging a ratio instead of recomputing it

Q45
When do you use Power Query and when do you use formulas?long answer
Answer
Formulas are live; Power Query is a recorded recipe. That is the whole distinction.

A formula sits in a cell and recalculates the instant anything it depends on changes. Power Query sits outside the sheet as a list of Applied Steps, and it runs when you press Refresh. The steps are the value: every transformation you did is written down, in order, visible and editable, and re-runnable on next month's file.

Use formulas for calculations that belong in the report — a bonus column, a variance against target, anything a reader should see the logic of. Use Power Query for everything that happens to the data before the report: importing, removing duplicates, splitting columns, fixing types, merging tables, appending files.

The practical test: if you will have to do it again next month, it belongs in Power Query. It also handles far more rows than the grid, since the result need not land on a sheet at all.
Must mention — tick these off
  1. Formulas recalculate live; Power Query runs on Refresh
  2. Applied Steps are a recorded, repeatable recipe
  3. Power Query for import and cleaning; formulas for report logic
  4. The test: will you do this again next month?
  5. Power Query handles more rows than the grid can hold
  6. Merge adds columns, Append adds rows — a join and a UNION ALL
  7. It is the same editor as Power BI, which is why it transfers
Probe if they stall. “Every month you get a new CSV in the same format and spend an hour cleaning it. Which tool, and what changes next month?” — Power Query; next month is one Refresh.
Sounds right, is wrong. “Power Query is just a fancier way to do what formulas do.” They solve different problems. A formula cannot remove duplicates or unpivot a table, and Power Query does not update until you ask it to.
Q46
Explain the join types in a merge, and which one you reach for first.long answer
Answer
Inner keeps only rows that matched on both sides. Left outer keeps every row from the first table and leaves nulls where there was no match — the one you want most of the time, because you keep all your records. Right outer is the mirror. Full outer keeps everything from both.

Then the two people forget. Left anti returns the rows of the first table that matched nothing, and right anti the reverse. These are the most useful joins for checking your data and they are the ones nobody runs.

On our file, joining 200 employees to a departments table that is missing Marketing gives: inner 158, left outer 200, right outer 159, full outer 201, left anti 42, right anti 1. The check that proves it: 158 + 42 = 200.

Run the anti join first. If it returns nothing, join with confidence. If it returns 42, you have learned something the inner join would have thrown away in silence.
Must mention — tick these off
  1. Inner, left outer, right outer, full outer
  2. Left anti and right anti — the rows that matched nothing
  3. Inner is the dangerous default — it drops rows without telling you
  4. Left outer keeps all your records and is usually what you want
  5. 158 + 42 = 200 — inner and left anti split the table exactly
  6. Run the anti join first as a data check
  7. The dialog tells you how many matched before you click OK
Probe if they stall. “The merge dialog says 'matches 158 of 200 rows'. What do you do with that sentence?” — read it before clicking OK; 42 employees were about to disappear.
Sounds right, is wrong. “Inner join is the safe default because it only keeps good data.” It keeps the overlap and tells you nothing about what it dropped. That is the opposite of safe.
Q47
How would you build a summary a manager can actually read?long answer
Answer
Start from the question, not from the data. What decision is this person about to make? Everything that does not help with it comes off the page.

Layout: headline numbers top-left, because that is where the eye lands. Breakdowns beside or under them. One chart, not six. A sentence at the bottom.

Every number comes from a formula, never typed, so it survives the data changing. Every number has a label a stranger can read. Money is formatted as money. Sheets have real names, not Sheet1.

And the part most people skip: the caveat. If two salaries are blank, the sheet says so. If one person is 27% of the salary bill, the sheet says that too, because the average on its own is misleading and the chart will not confess. A chart title should be a sentence — ‘Sales is the largest department at 64’, not ‘Headcount by department’, which the axis labels already told you.

Probe if they stall. “Write me the one sentence that goes at the bottom of our employee summary.” — it must contain a number and the caveat.

Q48
Explain conditional formatting, and tell me when it misleads.long answer
Answer
Conditional formatting applies a format when a rule is true — highlight cells over a threshold, data bars, colour scales, icon sets. Its value is speed of reading: a pattern in colour is found faster than a pattern in digits.

Where it misleads. Colour scales rescale themselves to whatever is on screen, so the same value is red in one view and green in another — and filtering changes the colours without changing a single number. Data bars are drawn from zero by default, so a bar twice as long is not twice the value unless the axis starts at zero and you have checked.

One extreme value flattens everything else. Put a colour scale on our salary column and the 52-lakh row takes the whole top of the scale, leaving 197 people in an indistinguishable band at the bottom. The rule is live, so it recalculates as the data changes — which is the feature, and also the trap when you screenshot it.
Must mention — tick these off

  2. Good for reading speed, not for precision
  3. Colour scales rescale to the visible data — filtering changes the colours
  4. Data bars start from zero by default — check before comparing lengths
  5. One outlier flattens the whole scale and hides real variation
  6. Rules on a whole column can be very slow on large sheets
  7. For precise comparison, sort or chart it instead of colouring it
Probe if they stall. “You put a colour scale on salary and almost every cell is the same shade. What is going on, and what do you do?”
Sounds right, is wrong. Treating colour intensity as a measurement. It is a ranking within whatever is currently visible, not a value you can read off.
Q49
Name three mistakes in Excel that produce no error message at all, and say how you would catch each one.long answer
Answer
One — the missing dollar signs. A formula that should point at one fixed cell gets filled down without $, and the reference walks. The whole column is wrong and nothing goes red. Catch it by clicking a formula halfway down the column and reading what it actually points at.

Two — sorting one column on its own. You select a single column, sort it, and every name is now attached to somebody else's salary. The file looks completely normal. Catch it by checking a row you know before and after, and by never sorting without selecting the whole table.

Three — VLOOKUP without FALSE. Approximate matching on unsorted data returns some right answers and some wrong ones with no pattern. Catch it by spot-checking three rows you can verify by hand.

If they want a fourth: a slicer connected to only one of four pivots, so the dashboard disagrees with itself while every number on it is individually correct.
Must mention — tick these off
  1. Missing $ → the reference walks when filled
  2. Sorting one column → rows are silently scrambled
  3. VLOOKUP without FALSE → approximate matches, no pattern
  4. Count of salary in a pivot instead of Sum or Average
  5. A slicer wired to one pivot of several
  6. Every catch is the same habit: check a value you already know
  7. Say the general lesson: no error message is not the same as no error

Choosing a chart

Q01
Which chart would you use, and why?
Answer
Bar — compare a value across categories. The safe default.
Line — change over time, where the order of the x axis means something.
Histogram — the distribution of one numeric column.
Box plot — spread and outliers, especially across groups.
Scatter — the relationship between two numeric columns.
Heatmap — a matrix of values, typically a correlation table.

Lead with the question, not the chart: comparison, trend, distribution, relationship, composition.
Q02
What is the difference between a bar chart and a histogram?
Answer
A bar chart compares categories, the bars have gaps, and the order can be rearranged freely. A histogram shows the distribution of one numeric column split into ranges, the bars touch, and the order is fixed because the x axis is a number line.

Asked constantly, and a surprising number of candidates get it wrong.
Q03
Why are pie charts usually a poor choice?
Answer
People judge angles and areas badly, so similar slices are hard to rank — a bar chart puts them on a common baseline and is read instantly.

A pie is defensible with very few slices, when the parts genuinely make a whole, and when the point is 'one slice dominates' rather than a precise comparison. More than about five slices and it is unreadable.
Q04
When would you use a stacked bar instead of a grouped bar?
Answer
Stacked when the total matters and the parts are secondary. Grouped when you want to compare the parts against each other.

The weakness of stacked bars is that only the bottom segment sits on a common baseline, so the middle segments are hard to compare across bars.

Reading and making them honest

Q05
What makes a chart misleading?
Answer
A truncated y axis — starting at 95 instead of 0 turns a 2% change into a cliff. The commonest one.
Inconsistent scales between charts being compared.
Cherry-picked date ranges.
Dual axes scaled to manufacture a correlation.
3D effects that distort area.
No sample size, so a percentage based on 10 people looks like one based on 10,000.

A bar chart should start at zero because the bar's length is the encoding. A line chart showing a trend need not.
Q06
What makes a chart finished?
Answer
A title that states the finding rather than the variables, both axes labelled with units, a legend only when there is more than one series, readable categories, and a note of the sample size.

The test: if you have to explain it out loud, the title is not doing its job. 'Employees by department' is adequate; 'Sales is a third of the company' is better.
Q07
You plot a histogram and it is one tall bar with empty space. What happened?
Answer
An extreme outlier has stretched the axis, so almost all the data is squeezed into the first bin.

Options: investigate the outlier first, then either cap or exclude it and say so, use a log scale, or plot the sensible range and annotate the outlier separately. The useless chart is itself the finding — it told you about the outlier.
Q08
What does a box plot show?
Answer
The five-number summary: minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum. The box is the middle 50% (the IQR), the line inside is the median, the whiskers reach the furthest points within 1.5×IQR, and anything beyond is drawn as a separate outlier dot.

It is the fastest way to compare spread across several groups at once, which a histogram cannot do.

Dashboards and BI tools

Q09
What makes a good dashboard?
Answer
Start from who reads it and what decision it supports. Then: the most important number first and largest, a small number of charts that each answer a stated question, consistent colour with meaning attached, filters that apply to everything, and a visible as-of date.

The common failure is a wall of charts because the data was available, rather than because somebody needed it.
Q10
How do Excel skills map onto Power BI?
Answer
Pivot table → a visual. SUMIF → a measure. Rows/Columns/Values/Filters → the field wells. Power Query is literally the same editor in both products.

So the analytical thinking transfers directly; what changes is the interface and the vocabulary. That is a strong answer when the job asks for Power BI and your background is Excel.
Q11
What is the difference between a calculated column and a measure?
Answer
A calculated column is computed row by row and stored in the table, using memory. A measure is computed on the fly in the context of whatever the user has filtered or grouped by.

Use a column when you need to slice or filter by the value; use a measure for aggregations like totals and averages, which is most of the time.
Q12
What is the value of Power Query over cleaning by hand?
Answer
It records every transformation as a named, re-runnable step. Clean by hand and the work exists only in your memory; clean in Power Query and next month's file runs through the same process, in order, by somebody who is not you.

It is also non-destructive — deleting a step undoes it, because the step was an instruction rather than an edit.

The chart decisions

Q13
Line or bar for data over time?
Answer
Line when the points are a continuous series and the trend between them is meaningful — daily revenue, monthly users. The line itself carries information: the slope.

Bar when the periods are discrete comparisons and you want people reading individual values — revenue in four quarters, headcount by year. Bars invite comparison of heights; lines invite reading a direction.

The test: does the space between two points mean anything? If joining January to February with a line implies something happened in between that you cannot support, use bars. With more than about fifteen periods a line almost always wins anyway, because fifteen bars is a fence.
Q14
What is a scatterplot for, and what would you add to one?
Answer
Two numeric variables, one point per record, to show the relationship — direction, strength, shape and the points that do not fit.

What to add, in order of value: a trend line so the eye is not doing the regression; transparency or smaller markers when points overplot, because a solid blob hides the density; a third variable as colour if it is categorical and has few levels.

What to say unprompted: a scatterplot is the chart that shows you why a correlation coefficient lies. Two variables can share r = 0.8 and look completely different — one clean line, one curve, one blob with a single far-off point dragging the number up. Always plot it before you quote the correlation.
Q15
What is a heatmap good for, and when is it the wrong choice?
Answer
Good for a matrix — two categorical axes with one number in each cell. A correlation matrix, sales by month and region, activity by hour and weekday. It shows pattern and clustering at a glance, which a table of the same numbers does not.

Wrong when the exact values matter, because people cannot read a number off a colour. The fix is to print the numbers in the cells and let the colour do the guiding — then it is a table that happens to be shaded, which is usually the best of both. Also wrong with a rainbow palette: use a single sequential ramp, or a diverging one with a meaningful midpoint if the values go both ways from zero.
Q16
When is a dual axis acceptable?
Answer
Rarely, and the honest answer leads with the problem: you choose the two scales, so you choose where the lines cross. Any 'relationship' a dual axis appears to show can be manufactured by rescaling one side, which makes it the easiest chart in the world to mislead with — usually by accident.

It is defensible when the two series are genuinely different units that a reader needs together and the shapes, not the crossings, are the message — revenue and conversion rate, say.

Better alternatives: index both to 100 at the start and use one axis; or stack two small charts with a shared x-axis. The second is almost always clearer and nobody can accuse you of anything.
Q17
Does a bar chart axis have to start at zero? What about a line chart?
Answer
Bar chart: yes. A bar encodes its value by length, so truncating the axis makes a 5% difference look like a doubling. This is the most common misleading chart there is.

Line chart: no. A line encodes change by position and slope, not length. Forcing a zero baseline on a stock price or a temperature series flattens the very variation you are trying to show.

The rule underneath both: start the axis at zero when the mark's size carries the meaning. If you do truncate, say so on the chart rather than hoping nobody checks.
Q18
How many colours should a chart use, and how do you choose them?
Answer
As few as the message needs. More than about six categorical colours and the legend becomes a lookup task — at that point group the tail into 'Other' or switch to small multiples.

Three rules worth stating:
• Grey is a colour. Colour the one series you are talking about, grey everything else. This single habit improves most charts more than any palette choice.
• Match the palette to the data. Sequential for a magnitude, diverging for deviation from a midpoint, categorical for unordered groups. A rainbow for a magnitude invents boundaries that are not in the data.
• Do not rely on colour alone. Around 8% of men have some colour-vision deficiency, and plenty of reports get printed in black and white. Vary line style or add direct labels too.
Q19
You have thirty categories to compare. What do you do?
Answer
Not a pie chart, and not thirty bars in alphabetical order.

In order of preference: sort by value and use a horizontal bar chart, which handles long labels and lets the reader stop when they stop caring. Keep the top 8 and group the rest into 'Other', labelled with how many it covers. Small multiples if each category has its own trend worth seeing. A sorted table with a sparkline or a shaded column, if the exact numbers matter more than the shape.

The judgement being tested is whether you will tell a stakeholder that their request for 'all of it on one chart' is the wrong ask, and offer something better.

Honesty and craft, continued

Q20
How do you show uncertainty in a chart?
Answer
Error bars or a shaded confidence band, and the real answer is that you do it at all. A forecast or a survey result drawn as a clean line reads as fact, and stakeholders act on it as fact.

For a comparison of two groups, overlapping intervals are the picture that stops a false conclusion before it is spoken — it is far more persuasive than quoting a p-value. For a forecast, a widening fan makes the obvious point that next month is more knowable than next year.

If the tooling cannot draw it, say it in the subtitle: ±3% at 95% confidence is better than silence.
Q21
What is faceting, or small multiples, and why is it underused?
Answer
One small chart per category, same axes throughout, laid out in a grid.

It is underused because it feels like giving up on a single clever chart. In fact it is usually the right answer when you have a third variable: comparing six lines on one axis is a tangle, while six small panels with identical scales lets the eye compare shapes instantly.

The one rule that makes or breaks it: the axes must be identical in every panel. Free scales make each panel look interesting and make the comparison meaningless, which is the default in some libraries — so check.
Q22
matplotlib or seaborn — when do you use which?
Answer
seaborn for the chart, matplotlib for the finishing. They are not competitors; seaborn is built on matplotlib and returns matplotlib objects.

seaborn gives you statistical charts in one line, understands a DataFrame and column names, and handles grouping, faceting and confidence intervals for you. matplotlib is where you go for exact control — titles, annotations, axis formatting, a layout nobody has thought of.

The working pattern is one seaborn call and then two or three matplotlib lines to label it properly. A candidate who says 'seaborn for exploring, then matplotlib to get it ready to send' has done this for real.
Q23
Explain the difference between a figure and an axes in matplotlib.
Answer
The figure is the whole canvas. An axes is one plot on it — its own x and y, its own title. One figure can hold many axes, which is how you build a grid.

Why it is asked: it separates people who use plt.something() for everything from people who understand what those calls do. plt.* quietly acts on 'the current axes', which is fine for one chart and falls apart the moment there are two. fig, ax = plt.subplots() and then calling methods on ax is the version that scales, and it is what seaborn hands you back.

Note the naming trap: an axes is a whole subplot, while an axis is one of its two directions. Confusingly similar, and worth getting right out loud.
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4))
ax.bar(df['dept'], df['headcount'])
ax.set_title('Headcount by department')
ax.set_ylabel('People')
Q24
What is the difference between a report and a dashboard?
Answer
A report answers a question. A dashboard monitors a state.

A report is written once, has a narrative and a conclusion, and ends with a recommendation. It is read and then filed. A dashboard is read repeatedly by people checking whether anything has changed, so it needs no narrative but it does need to make 'normal' obvious at a glance.

The practical consequence: most requests for a dashboard are really requests for a report, and building the wrong one wastes weeks. The question to ask is 'how often will you look at this, and what will you do differently depending on what it says?' If there is no recurring decision, it is a report.
Q25
In Power BI, what is a star schema and why does it matter?
Answer
One central fact table of events — orders, transactions — joined to several small dimension tables that describe them: date, customer, product.

Why it matters in a BI tool specifically: the model drives everything. A star schema makes filters propagate predictably from the dimensions down to the facts, keeps the DAX simple, and performs well. The alternative people arrive with — one giant flat table, or a web of many-to-many relationships — produces measures that give different answers depending on which slicer is set, which is the hardest class of bug to explain to a stakeholder.

The link back to the SQL syllabus: this is normalisation applied with a purpose. You normalise the descriptions into dimensions and deliberately leave the fact table long and narrow.
Q26
What is row context versus filter context in Power BI?
Answer
The thing that trips everyone moving from Excel to DAX, and the reason a calculated column and a measure give different answers.

Row context — 'the current row'. A calculated column has it, which is why [price] * [qty] works there like a filled-down Excel formula.
Filter context — 'the set of rows the visual has left in scope' after slicers, rows, columns and page filters. A measure is evaluated in filter context, which is why the same measure gives a different number in each cell of a matrix and why it recalculates when you click a slicer.

The one-line version: a column is computed once when the data loads; a measure is computed every time you look at it, against whatever is currently filtered.

The round that decides offers once the technical rounds are a tie. Process, judgement, and explaining a finding to somebody who does not want the detail.

How you work

Q01
Walk me through how you would approach a new dataset.
Answer
A sequence, said in order — the structure is what is being assessed:

1. Understand the question before opening the file. What decision does this support?
2. Understand the data — shape, columns, types, what one row represents.
3. Assess quality — missing values, duplicates, impossible values, ranges that make no sense.
4. Clean, recording what you changed and why.
5. Explore — distributions first, then relationships.
6. Analyse against the original question.
7. Communicate — the finding, the caveat, the recommendation.

Mentioning that you check what one row represents is a strong early signal.
Q02
How do you handle missing data?
Answer
First ask why it is missing, because that drives the fix. Missing at random is very different from missing because a system failed for one region all of March.

Then choose: drop the rows if there are few and they are not systematic; drop the column if most of it is empty; impute with median (safer than mean when skewed), mode for categories, or forward-fill for time series; or keep it as its own category, since 'not provided' is sometimes informative.

Whatever you choose, state it in the output. There is no correct answer, only a declared one.
Q03
How do you make sure your analysis is correct?
Answer
Concrete checks beat 'I double-check it':

Totals reconcile — the group counts add back to the row count.
Row counts before and after every join and filter.
Sanity-check against a known number somebody else already reported.
Spot-check a few records by hand.
Ask whether the answer is plausible — a 400% month-on-month jump is usually a pipeline bug.

The theme: a wrong answer in analytics usually looks exactly like a right one, so you need checks that would catch it.
Q04
Tell me about a time your analysis was wrong, or you found an error.
Answer
Use a real example and a clear structure: what you found, how you found it, what you did, what you changed afterwards.

What is being tested is whether you own it and have a system. Strong answers end with a process change — 'I now check row counts before and after every join' — rather than 'I was more careful next time'.

Trainer note: a candidate who says they have never made a mistake is either inexperienced or not checking.
Q05
You are given a question you think is the wrong question. What do you do?
Answer
Answer it, and then say why you think a different question is better — with evidence.

Refusing looks obstructive and answering silently wastes everybody's time. 'Here is the number you asked for. I also looked at X, because the thing you actually seem to be deciding is Y' is the response that gets analysts promoted.

Judgement

Q06
Sales dropped 20% last month. How would you investigate?
Answer
Do not guess a cause. Narrow it down.

1. Verify it. Is the drop real, or did the pipeline break, a region stop reporting, or the definition change?
2. Is it unusual? Compare with the same month last year — it may be seasonal.
3. Segment. Split by region, product, channel, customer type. A drop that is uniform means something broad; one concentrated in a single segment is a much smaller problem to find.
4. Decompose. Revenue is orders × value; which moved?
5. Check for external causes — a competitor, a price change, a holiday shift.

Then report what you found, what you ruled out, and what you still do not know.
Q07
A stakeholder says your number is wrong. How do you respond?
Answer
Assume they may be right. Ask what number they expected and where theirs came from — most disputes are a definition mismatch, not an arithmetic error. Different date range, different filter, different idea of what counts as 'active'.

Then walk through your logic, compare definitions, and agree one. Being defensive loses trust even when you are correct.
Q08
How do you decide which metric to use?
Answer
Start from the decision it drives. Then check that it is sensitive to what you are changing, hard to game, defined unambiguously, and available reliably.

Prefer rates to raw counts when the base changes. Be wary of averages on skewed data — a median is often the honest choice. And pair a primary metric with a guardrail, so you notice if you improve one thing by breaking another.
Q09
Your result is statistically significant but tiny. What do you report?
Answer
Both facts, in that order: the effect is real, and it is small. With a large enough sample almost anything reaches significance.

Then give the effect size in business terms — '0.2% more conversions, about 40 orders a month' — and let the reader decide whether that justifies the cost. Reporting significance alone, without magnitude, is one of the most common analyst mistakes.
Q10
How would you explain a 24% weekend-joining rate in HR data?
Answer
Treat it as a data-quality signal, not a finding. No company deliberately starts a quarter of its staff at a weekend.

So: confirm the calculation, then look upstream — are these backfilled records, a default date, a system that stamps the import date rather than the real one? Check whether they cluster around particular dates or sources.

The general principle: distinguish interesting from impossible. An unusual number is worth investigating; a number that should not logically exist is a bug until proven otherwise.

Communication

Q11
How do you explain a technical finding to a non-technical stakeholder?
Answer
Lead with the answer, not the method. They want the conclusion and what to do about it.

Then: use their vocabulary, not yours; quantify in money or customers rather than percentages alone; use one chart rather than a table; state the caveat plainly; and keep the methodology available but not in the room.

Structure that works: what we found → why it matters → what I recommend → what I am unsure about. 'The mean is skewed by an outlier' becomes 'one employee earns fifty-two lakh, which pulls the average up by about 27,000'.
Q12
What is the difference between a number and a finding?
Answer
'Average salary is ₹95,830' is a number. 'Average salary is ₹95,830, but one employee earns ₹52 lakh, so the median of ₹68,841 is the better figure to plan with' is a finding.

Both are true; only one is useful. The job is not to calculate — the tool does that — it is to say what the number means and what should be done about it.
Q13
How would you present a result you know the stakeholder will not like?
Answer
Lead with it rather than burying it, and bring the evidence and the caveats in the same breath. Separate what the data says from what you recommend, so disagreement about the recommendation does not become disagreement about the facts.

Offer what would change your mind — 'if we had three more months of data, this could look different' — which is honest and keeps the conversation open.
Q14
What would you put in a one-page summary?
Answer
A title that states the finding, the date, the number of records it covers, two or three numbers that matter, one chart, and at least one caveat.

Test it by asking: could somebody open this with no context and know what matters, without asking me a question? If they need you in the room, it is not finished.
Q15
Why do you want to be a data analyst?
Answer
Trainer note: the content matters less than whether it is specific. Generic answers about 'loving data' are forgettable.

Good answers connect something concrete the candidate did — a project, a dataset they cleaned, a question they chased — to what the role actually involves. Push for a specific example if the first answer is abstract.
Q16
Do you have any questions for us?
Answer
Trainer note: never 'no'. Coach candidates to prepare three.

Good ones: what does a typical week look like; what tools does the team actually use day to day; who consumes the analysis and how are decisions made from it; what would success look like in the first six months. These show the candidate is assessing the role, which reads as confidence.

Something broke

Q17
Conversion rate dropped from 4.2% to 3.1% overnight. Walk me through your investigation.
Answer
The single most common analytics scenario. The method matters more than the answer, and the order matters most of all.

1. Is it real? Before anything else, check the data. A tracking tag removed, a pipeline that half-loaded, a bot filter switched on, a definition changed. An overnight step change in a ratio is more often an instrumentation problem than a business one, and investigating a measurement artefact for a day is embarrassing.
2. Numerator or denominator? A rate has two halves. Did purchases fall, or did traffic rise? Those are opposite stories and people skip straight past this. A cheap campaign bringing unqualified traffic lowers conversion while increasing revenue.
3. Segment it. Device, channel, geography, new versus returning, browser, app version. A drop concentrated in one segment is a bug; a drop spread evenly across all of them is a market or a measurement change.
4. Walk the funnel. Find the step where the fall-off happens. That usually names the cause by itself.
5. What changed? Releases, prices, campaigns, a competitor, a payment provider outage.

Then report what you know, what you suspect and what you need to confirm it — separately.
Q18
Two dashboards show different revenue for the same month. How do you reconcile them?
Answer
Do not start by looking for the bug. Start by asking what each number is defined as, because most of the time neither is wrong — they are answering different questions.

The usual suspects, in the order they occur: date basis (order date, ship date, invoice date, payment date), status filter (are cancelled and refunded orders included), gross versus net of tax, discount and shipping, currency and the rate used, timezone at the month boundary, and late-arriving data if one was refreshed earlier than the other.

Then reconcile numerically rather than by argument: start from one number and add or subtract each difference until you land on the other. A reconciliation table that accounts for every rupee of the gap ends the conversation; an opinion about which system is better does not.

Finish by fixing the cause, which is nearly always that the definition was never written down. Agree one, document it, and point both dashboards at the same source.
Q19
You discover a mistake in a report you sent to leadership last week. What do you do?
Answer
Tell them, promptly, yourself. That is the answer, and how you say it is what is being assessed.

Work out the impact before you speak so you can lead with it: what the number should have been, whether it changes the conclusion, and whether any decision was made on it. 'Revenue was ₹4.2 crore, not ₹4.6 crore — the growth trend and the recommendation are unchanged' is a very different message from 'the recommendation reverses', and the recipient needs to know which immediately.

Then: correct the artefact, say plainly what caused it, and say what you have changed so it cannot recur — a check in the pipeline, a reconciliation step, a second pair of eyes on anything that goes to the board.

What loses the offer is hoping nobody notices, burying it in a footnote, or blaming the source system without owning that you published it. Everybody ships a wrong number eventually; this question is about what happens next.
Q20
The overnight pipeline failed and today's data is only partially loaded. The daily report goes out in an hour. What do you do?
Answer
Do not send a partial number without a label. A report that silently covers 14 hours instead of 24 will be read as a catastrophic drop, and someone will act on it.

In order: establish exactly what is missing and whether it is a clean cut-off or scattered gaps. Hold the report if you can and say when it will come. If it must go, send it clearly marked — partial data, covers to 14:00, totals understated, full figures tomorrow at 09:00 — and suppress or grey out the charts that are actively misleading rather than just incomplete.

Then the follow-up the interviewer wants: the real fix is that the report should not have been able to render silently on partial data. A freshness check and a row-count assertion that fails loudly beats a human noticing. Saying that unprompted shows you think about systems, not just today's fire.
Q21
A column you need is 40% missing. What now?
Answer
Ask why it is missing before deciding what to do, because the mechanism determines the honest options.

If it is missing at random — a form field people skipped — the remaining 60% may still be representative. If it is missing systematically, it is not: a salary field blank for all contractors, a feedback score only from people who complained. There the missingness is itself the finding, and analysing the 60% gives you a confident, wrong answer.

The options, with what each costs: drop the rows (fine at 2%, destroys the dataset at 40%); drop the column and say what you lost; impute, which fabricates precision and shrinks variance, so only with a group-wise estimate and a documented note; add a 'missing' category and let it be a level in its own right, which is often the most honest choice; go and get the data, which nobody suggests and is sometimes easy.

At 40%, the answer that would worry me is a confident fillna(mean).

Judgement under pressure

Q22
Your A/B test shows no significant difference. What do you report?
Answer
Not 'the test failed', and not 'there is no difference'.

First check whether the test could have detected the effect you cared about. An underpowered test returning nothing is uninformative, not negative — and if you did not calculate the sample size beforehand, that is the finding. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

What to report: the observed difference with its confidence interval. '+0.3%, interval −1.1% to +1.7%' is genuinely useful — it rules out the large effect that was hoped for. That is a real answer, and it is why reporting an interval beats reporting a yes/no.

Then the decision framing: if the change costs nothing to keep and the interval excludes harm, ship it. If it carries cost or complexity, do not. A null result that stops the team building on a false premise has paid for itself.
Q23
The test winner improved clicks but reduced average order value. Which do you recommend?
Answer
The question is not statistical, it is about which metric the business is actually trying to move, and the right answer begins by refusing the frame. Clicks are not the goal; revenue or profit is.

Compute the combined effect: more clicks times a smaller basket may be up, down or flat, and that single number usually settles it. Then check whether the AOV fall is composition — the variant attracted more low-intent buyers — rather than existing buyers spending less, because those have different long-run consequences.

Say explicitly that the secondary metric moving is a guardrail breach and is exactly why guardrails are defined before a test. And flag the honest limit: a short test cannot see retention or returns, so a win on revenue today can still be a loss. Recommend with a stated horizon and something to monitor after launch.
Q24
A stakeholder asks you to prove that their new campaign worked.
Answer
Handle it without being difficult about it. The ask, taken literally, is to confirm a conclusion — which is how you produce an analysis that gets contradicted by reality a quarter later, with your name on it.

Reframe rather than refuse: 'Let me measure the campaign's effect — here is how I will do it and what would count as working.' Agreeing the success criterion and the comparison before you look is the part that protects both of you.

Then be concrete about the method, because 'revenue went up after the campaign' is not evidence: what is the counterfactual? A holdout group, a matched region, a pre-post comparison with seasonality accounted for, a difference-in-differences. Name the confounders — the campaign ran into the festival season, a price change landed the same week.

And deliver it as a magnitude with uncertainty, not a verdict. 'Between 3% and 9% incremental, most likely 6%' is both more useful and more defensible than 'yes, it worked'.
Q25
You are asked for a metric you know will be gamed. What do you say?
Answer
Say it, early, with a specific prediction of how. Vague concern gets dismissed; 'if we target average handling time, calls will get transferred instead of resolved, and repeat contact rate will rise' is a testable claim that changes the conversation.

The principle is Goodhart's law: a measure that becomes a target stops measuring the thing. It is not an argument against metrics, it is an argument for pairing them — speed with resolution quality, volume with error rate, sales with refunds. A metric with a counterweight is much harder to game than one on its own.

Then accept the decision if it goes against you. Ship the metric, ship the guardrail beside it, and make sure the guardrail is on the same dashboard. Being right about this a year later is worth nothing if you refused to help in the meantime.
Q26
Your analysis contradicts a decision leadership has already announced.
Answer
Check yourself first, properly — data, logic, assumptions — and get a colleague to look. A contrarian finding attracts scrutiny, so it needs to be the most carefully verified thing you have produced.

Then take it privately to the person who owns the decision, before any wider forum. Ambushing someone with it in a meeting makes the conversation about their position rather than the evidence.

Lead with the decision implication, not the method, and be precise about how strong it is: 'this changes the payback period from 9 months to 2 years — here is the analysis, here is what would have to be true for the original case to hold.' Giving them the conditions under which they were right is both honest and far more persuasive than a flat contradiction.

Accept that the decision may stand for reasons you cannot see. Your job is to make sure it is made with the information, not to win.
Q27
The CEO asks you for a number in a meeting and you are not certain of it.
Answer
Say you will confirm it, and give the range you are confident in. 'Around 40,000, and I will have the exact figure within the hour' is a completely acceptable answer. Guessing precisely is not, because a specific number said in a meeting gets repeated and then quoted back to you as fact.

What makes this answer good rather than evasive is that it is bounded and it comes with a commitment. Then honour it, in writing, to everyone who was in the room — including the correction if the real number differs.

The habit underneath, worth stating: know your three or four headline numbers cold. Being asked for monthly active users or last month's revenue and having no idea is a different and worse problem than uncertainty about a detail.

Defining and measuring

Q28
How would you define an 'active user'?
Answer
Start by refusing to answer in the abstract: it depends on what the product is for and what decision the number will drive. A banking app and a social network have completely different natural rhythms.

Then show the structure of a good definition. What action counts — opening the app, or doing something of value? Login-based counts are the easiest to game and the least useful. Over what window — daily, weekly, monthly, and the DAU/MAU ratio as a stickiness measure. Who is excluded — internal users, bots, test accounts.

Two things that mark out experience. Say that the definition must be written down and versioned, because the number is meaningless across time if the definition drifts — and a redefinition that creates a step change in a chart must be annotated on it. And note that you cannot change it retroactively without restating history, so it is worth getting roughly right at the start.
Q29
How would you measure whether a new feature is successful?
Answer
Before the data: what was it supposed to do? A feature built to increase retention and a feature built to reduce support load succeed differently, and a surprising number of launches have never had this written down.

Then a small set of metrics, deliberately layered:
• Adoption — what share of eligible users tried it, and did they come back to it? Usage that spikes and dies is a failed feature with good launch numbers.
• The target outcome — the one metric it was built to move. Pick it in advance, pick one.
• Guardrails — what must not get worse. Latency, support tickets, churn, revenue.
• Segments — a feature that helps power users and confuses new ones nets to zero and needs a different decision from one that helps nobody.

And how you will attribute it: a staged rollout or holdout gives you a real comparison, where a before-and-after does not. Say what you would need in place before launch, because that is the part teams skip and then cannot recover.
Q30
Customer churn is rising. How do you find out why?
Answer
First make the number mean something. Define churn — cancelled, or merely inactive for 90 days? — and check that the definition has not changed. Then confirm it is rising in rate, not just in count: more churned customers with many more customers may be a falling rate.

Then the one analysis that answers this better than any other: a cohort table. Group customers by when they joined and track retention by month of life. It separates the two completely different problems that a single churn number confuses — are recent cohorts worse than older ones (an acquisition or onboarding problem) or are all cohorts getting worse at the same calendar moment (a price change, a competitor, a product regression).

After that, segment the churners by plan, channel, geography and tenure; compare their behaviour before leaving against those who stayed; and read the cancellation reasons and some support tickets. Qualitative data is where the hypothesis comes from, and ten customer calls will often beat a week of modelling.
Q31
How would you forecast next quarter's revenue?
Answer
Start with the simplest thing that could work and only add complexity that earns its place. Last quarter, adjusted for seasonality, is a baseline that is embarrassingly hard to beat, and you should always state it so there is something to compare against.

Then decompose rather than model the total: revenue is customers × orders per customer × average order value. Forecasting the three pieces separately is usually more accurate and far more useful, because each one is something a team can act on.

Then the things that matter more than the technique: use year-on-year comparisons so seasonality is handled; incorporate what you know is coming that the history cannot contain — a signed contract, a price rise, a campaign; and give a range, not a number, because a point forecast will be wrong and a range can be right.

Finish by saying you would backtest it on history you held out, and track the error afterwards. A forecast nobody scores is a guess with a spreadsheet attached.
Q32
Roughly how many auto-rickshaws are there in Bengaluru?
Answer
A guesstimate, and nobody is checking your answer against a register. They are checking that you decompose, state assumptions, and sanity-check rather than freeze or guess a number.

A demand-side route: Bengaluru has roughly 1.3 crore people. Say one in twelve takes an auto on a given day, so about 11 lakh trips. An auto does maybe 20 trips a day, giving around 55,000 autos. Round to 50,000 to 80,000.

Then the part that actually earns the marks: say which assumption your answer is most sensitive to — trips per auto per day, by a long way — and sanity-check against something you know, such as whether that implies a plausible number of autos per thousand people. Offer an independent cross-check too: permit counts, or autos visible per kilometre of main road.

Say the numbers out loud, keep them round, and do not reach for a calculator.
Q33
Two teams define revenue differently and both are in the board pack. How do you fix it?
Answer
This is a governance problem wearing an analysis costume, so do not solve it with a better query.

Short term: reconcile them line by line, show the board one number with the differences itemised beneath, and label each existing figure with its definition so nobody has to guess which is which.

Long term, the fix is a single agreed definition with a named owner, written in a place people can find, and then computed once in a shared layer that both teams consume. Two teams writing their own SQL for 'revenue' will diverge again within a quarter, however good the meeting was.

The non-technical half, which is the real work: the two definitions usually exist because both teams need different things — finance needs recognised revenue, sales needs booked. The answer is often two clearly named metrics, not one compromise metric that serves neither. Naming them distinctly is a better outcome than forcing agreement.

Working with people

Q34
You have five requests and time for two. How do you decide?
Answer
Not first-come-first-served, and not loudest-voice. Ask two questions of each: what decision does this inform, and when is it made? A request that cannot name a decision is usually curiosity, and curiosity is fine but it waits.

Then rank on impact against effort, and be honest that a 20-minute answer that is 80% right often beats a two-day answer. Several requests are frequently the same question, and two of the five can be served by one piece of work.

The part people miss is the communication: tell everyone where they are in the queue and when to expect something. Most frustration comes from silence, not from waiting. And take the prioritisation to your manager rather than deciding alone when the trade-off is between two teams — that is their call, and it protects you from being the person who said no.
Q35
You inherit an important report with no documentation and the author has left.
Answer
Resist the urge to rebuild it. Start by finding out who uses it and for what — you may discover that two of the seven tabs matter and the rest can be retired, which is the cheapest possible outcome.

Then verify before you touch anything: reproduce last period's published numbers exactly. Until you can do that, you do not understand the report, and any change you make is unauditable.

Then work outwards from the outputs — trace each number back to its source, write down the logic as you go, and note the surprises, because an odd filter or a hard-coded exclusion is usually a real business rule nobody wrote down. Ask about those rather than removing them.

Document as you learn, not at the end. And resist the second urge too: a rewrite that changes the numbers, however much more correct, will be received as having broken the report unless you explain the differences first.
Q36
A stakeholder wants a daily dashboard but the data is far too noisy to read daily.
Answer
Do not just say no — the underlying need is real, which is wanting to know quickly if something is wrong.

Show them the problem rather than describing it: plot the daily series and the 7-day average together. Once someone sees that daily swings of ±15% are normal, the argument makes itself and they stop asking why Tuesday was down.

Then serve the actual need. Give daily data with a rolling average as the headline and the raw points faint behind it. Add control limits so 'unusual' is defined rather than eyeballed. And if the real requirement is early warning, an alert on a threshold is a better product than a dashboard somebody has to remember to open.

The general principle, worth saying: match the reporting frequency to how fast the metric can actually move and how fast anyone can act on it. Daily reporting on a metric that responds monthly manufactures false alarms, and false alarms get dashboards ignored.
Q37
Your stakeholder asks for 'all the data' in one spreadsheet.
Answer
Usually a sign that previous reports did not answer their question, so they want to go and look themselves. Treat that as useful information rather than an annoyance.

Ask what they are trying to find out. Nine times out of ten there is a specific question behind it, and answering that is faster for both of you. Sometimes there genuinely is exploratory work to do, and then the right answer is access to a filtered, documented view rather than a file.

If you do send an extract, make it safe: only the columns needed, no personal data that is not required, a date in the filename, a tab stating what it covers and when it was pulled, and a note that it is a snapshot. A 300,000-row untitled file becomes the source of three contradictory numbers within a month, and you will be asked to explain all three.

The better offer is usually: 'let me build you something you can filter yourself'.
Q38
What would you do in your first month in this role?
Answer
A question about whether you will be useful quickly without breaking things.

Weeks 1 to 2 — learn the business and the data. What the company makes money from, what the three numbers everyone watches are, and how they are defined. Meet the people who consume the reports and ask what they do not trust. Find out where the data lives, what is already built, and what everyone knows is broken.

Weeks 3 to 4 — deliver something small. Pick a real request, ship it properly, and ask for feedback. Early credibility comes from one useful thing finished, not from a plan for a platform.

What I would deliberately not do: propose rebuilding the stack, or redefine a metric before I understand why it is the way it is. Most odd things in a mature reporting setup are load-bearing.

Then two honest questions back — what would make the first three months a success for you, and what is the thing everybody complains about?
Q39
A customer table has rows you suspect are the same person. How do you decide?
Answer
A deduplication question, and the right first move is to establish that there is no objectively correct answer — the business has to define identity. Your job is to make the choice visible and defensible.

Work in layers. Exact matches on a reliable key first — the same email, the same phone, the same tax id. Then normalised matches: trim, lowercase, strip punctuation, standardise 'Pvt Ltd' against 'Private Limited'. Then fuzzy matching on names and addresses, which finds real duplicates and also real distinct records, so it needs a threshold and review.

Three things that mark out care. Count the impact before deciding: if merging changes customer count by 0.4% nobody needs a project. Never delete — flag a surviving record and keep the links, because the merge will be wrong sometimes and you will need to undo it. And check the asymmetry of the error: wrongly merging two companies into one is usually far worse than leaving two records for the same company, so set the threshold accordingly.

Then say that the real fix is validation at entry, which is where the duplicates came from.

The round that decides more offers than any technical question, and the one candidates prepare least. The answers below are not scripts to hand over — they describe what a good answer contains, what to probe for, and the red flags. Most answers here should be built on STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result — with the Result carrying a number wherever one exists.

Opening questions

Q01
Tell me about yourself.
Answer
Almost always the first question and almost always answered badly — either as a recital of the CV or as a life story starting from school.

A good answer is 60 to 90 seconds with three parts: where they are now and what they can do, one or two concrete things they have built with the result, and why that leads to this role. It should end pointed at the job, not trail off.

What to coach: lead with the strongest relevant thing, not with chronology. Name tools only alongside what they were used for — 'I used SQL and pandas to build a churn summary that the sales team now uses weekly' beats a list of six technologies.

Red flags: more than two minutes; starting with 'I was born in'; marks and percentages with no project; no mention of the company or role at the end.
Q02
Why data analytics?
Answer
The interviewer is testing whether the interest is real or whether analytics was simply the course available. They have heard 'data is the new oil' and 'I like working with data' many times, and neither answer survives a follow-up.

A good answer is specific and personal: a moment when a number changed their mind, a small thing they analysed because they wanted to know, the part of the work they find genuinely satisfying. Then one sentence connecting it to why they stuck with it through the boring parts, because cleaning data is most of the job.

Probe with 'what was the last thing you analysed that nobody asked you to?' The answer to that separates interest from positioning, and it is a fair question because a small self-started project is within reach of anybody on a laptop.
Q03
Walk me through a project on your resume.
Answer
The most important question in the behavioural round, because every claim on the CV is tested through it. Expect to spend ten minutes here.

A good answer covers: the question the project was answering, where the data came from and how messy it was, what they actually did (and what they decided to leave out), what they found, and what changed as a result. The last part is what makes it a project rather than an exercise.

Then probe hard and specifically, because this is where exaggeration shows: how many rows? what did you do about the missing values, and why that? what was the hardest bug? what would you do differently? which part did you write yourself?

Red flags: cannot say the data size or source; describes a tutorial dataset as original work; every number is round; 'we' throughout with no account of their own part; no limitations acknowledged.
Q04
What was the hardest part of that project?
Answer
A deliberately open follow-up, and the answer reveals the altitude the person worked at.

A weak answer names an installation or environment problem — a library that would not install, a laptop that was slow. A good answer names a judgement: the data did not support the question as asked, two sources disagreed and they had to decide which to trust, the first approach gave an implausible result and they had to work out why.

What you are listening for is whether they noticed something was wrong. Anyone can follow a pipeline to the end; the job is catching the point at which the output stopped making sense.

Good probe: 'what made you suspect the first answer was wrong?'

Behaviour and teamwork

Q05
Tell me about a time you disagreed with someone.
Answer
Testing whether they can hold a position without being difficult, and whether they can be wrong gracefully. Both matter in a role whose whole output is telling people things they did not expect.

A good answer has a real disagreement with substance, their reasoning stated, the fact that they listened to the other side, and a resolution — which may well be that they were persuaded. 'I showed them the data and they agreed' every time is less convincing than one example where they changed their own mind.

Red flags: 'I have never disagreed with anyone', which reads as either passive or untruthful; criticising a named former colleague or manager; a story where being right is the whole point and the relationship afterwards is not mentioned.

Probe: 'what did they say that you hadn't considered?'
Q06
Tell me about a time you failed, or missed a deadline.
Answer
The question is about ownership, and the trap is a non-answer — a 'failure' that is secretly a success, or a cause located entirely outside themselves.

A good answer names something that actually went wrong, takes a fair share of responsibility, says what the consequence was, and — the part that matters — names the specific change in how they work since. 'I now agree the output format before starting' is a real lesson; 'I learned to manage time better' is not.

Red flags: 'my weakness is that I work too hard' territory; blaming teammates or the dataset with no self-examination; a failure so trivial that nothing was at stake.

This question is also the single best preparation to coach, because almost every candidate is caught unprepared by it and the recovery is visible.
Q07
Tell me about a time you had to learn something quickly.
Answer
Analytics tools change, so this is a genuine requirement rather than a filler question.

A good answer is concrete about what they had to learn, why there was time pressure, how they went about it, and what the outcome was. The 'how' is the interesting part: documentation, a small test case, asking someone, building the smallest working version first. Someone who says 'I watched a course' and nothing else has described consumption, not learning.

What marks out a strong answer is knowing when to stop learning and start building — learning the 20% needed for the task and returning to the rest later is exactly the right instinct under a deadline.

Probe: 'what did you decide not to learn?'
Q08
Tell me about a time you explained something technical to someone non-technical.
Answer
The most job-relevant behavioural question there is, because an analyst's work only counts once someone else acts on it.

A good answer names who the audience was, what they cared about, and the specific choice made to bridge the gap — an analogy, a chart instead of a table, dropping the method entirely and leading with the implication. Then evidence that it worked: a decision taken, a question answered, the person using it afterwards unaided.

Listen for whether they led with the conclusion. Candidates who narrate their method first and arrive at the finding last are describing how they worked, not how they communicated, and that is the habit this question is designed to expose.

Red flag: any hint that the audience was the problem.
Q09
Tell me about a time you took initiative.
Answer
Testing whether they operate beyond the ticket they were handed — which is most of the difference between a junior and a mid-level analyst.

A good answer has them noticing something nobody pointed out: a report that was wrong, a manual process that could be automated, a question the data could answer that nobody had asked. Then acting, and ideally checking with someone first rather than going quiet for two weeks.

The result should be modest and real. Automating a weekly two-hour task is a perfectly good answer and more believable than a transformation of the business.

Red flag: initiative that was really going around someone. Ask 'who did you tell before you started?' — the answer separates initiative from a loose cannon.
Q10
How do you handle pressure and competing deadlines?
Answer
Everybody says they work well under pressure, so the answer only counts if it describes a mechanism.

Good answers contain something checkable: how they list and rank work, that they ask which of two requests matters more rather than silently choosing, that they flag a slipping deadline early rather than on the day, that they will ship an 80% answer when that is what the deadline allows and say which parts are provisional.

The single most valuable thing to hear is raising a problem before it becomes one. A junior analyst who says on Tuesday that Friday is at risk is far more useful than one who works heroically and misses it anyway.

Follow up with a real example, because the general answer is easy to rehearse.

Fit, money and closing

Q11
What are your strengths and weaknesses?
Answer
The weakness half is the whole question. A fake weakness — perfectionism, working too hard, caring too much — signals someone managing you rather than talking to you, and most interviewers register it immediately.

A good answer names something real but not disqualifying, and pairs it with what they are doing about it. 'My Excel was much weaker than my Python, so I spent a month working through pivot tables and Power Query properly' is honest, specific and shows self-correction. Presentation nerves, a tendency to go too deep before checking the requirement, inexperience with a specific tool — all fine.

For strengths: two at most, each attached to an example. A list of six adjectives carries no information.

Red flag: a 'weakness' that is central to the job, with no plan.
Q12
Why do you want to work here?
Answer
A test of whether they have done twenty minutes of homework, and a surprising number have not.

A good answer names something specific and checkable about the company — what it does, the market it is in, a product, something it published — and connects it to what the candidate wants to do. Two sentences of genuine specificity beats a paragraph of praise.

It is also fine and often refreshing to name an honest practical reason alongside: the scale of the data, the chance to work across the whole pipeline, the location, a team that does the kind of work they want to learn.

Red flags: an answer that would fit any employer; flattery with no content; getting a basic fact about the company wrong, which is worse than saying less.
Q13
Where do you see yourself in three years?
Answer
Testing for direction and plausibility, not for a life plan. The interviewer is also quietly checking whether the role is a stepping stone they will be abandoning in six months.

A good answer shows a direction that grows through this job rather than away from it: deeper technical skill, ownership of a reporting area, mentoring newer analysts, moving towards a specialism the company actually has. Ambition is positive; it just has to be reachable from the chair they are applying for.

Red flags: 'I want to be a data scientist' said in a way that dismisses the analyst role, as it often is — coach them to frame it as building the foundation rather than passing through; naming a job title two levels up with no path; 'I have not thought about it'; or anything suggesting they plan to leave for higher study without saying so.
Q14
What are your salary expectations?
Answer
Mostly a composure question, and candidates either freeze or undersell themselves badly.

The right shape: a researched range rather than a single number, framed as negotiable and tied to the role rather than to need. 'Based on what I have seen for this role in this city, I am looking at X to Y, and I am open to discussing the whole package' is a complete answer. Asking what the band for the role is, before naming a number, is also perfectly legitimate.

What to coach specifically for freshers: do the research, so the range is defensible; do not name a figure far below market hoping it helps, because it rarely does and it anchors every later conversation; and never justify a number by personal expenses.

Red flags: 'whatever you think is fair', which hands over the negotiation; a number with no basis; refusing to engage at all.
Q15
Are you willing to relocate? What is your notice period?
Answer
Administrative, and the only wrong answer is a vague one. A clear yes, a clear no, or a specific condition — all fine. 'Maybe, I will have to see' creates doubt about everything else.

Notice period and availability should be stated exactly, in weeks, along with anything that affects it. If there are other offers in play it is better to say so neutrally than to be discovered later.

The one thing worth coaching: do not say yes to something you will not do. Agreeing to relocate and then declining the offer over it costs the candidate the reference and costs the company a month, and it happens often enough that interviewers ask twice.
Q16
Is there anything you would like to ask us?
Answer
Never 'no'. An empty answer here reads as indifference and it is the last impression in the room.

Two or three questions is right, and the best ones are about the work: what the first three months would involve, what the data stack is and where the gaps are, how requests reach the analytics team and who prioritises them, who consumes the output, how success in the role is judged. Those get genuine answers and they signal someone imagining the job rather than the offer.

Fair to ask about learning support and about team structure. Leave salary and leave policy for the HR conversation unless invited.

Coach one practical habit: have the questions written down, and have four, because two of them will get answered during the interview.
